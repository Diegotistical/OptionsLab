\begin{abstract}
Volatility surface calibration faces a forced choice: parametric models (SVI, SSVI) guarantee no-arbitrage but suffer order-of-magnitude accuracy degradation under sparse strike grids; neural networks interpolate flexibly but produce exploitable butterfly and calendar arbitrage. \textbf{We close this accuracy-arbitrage gap under realistic sparsity.}

We train a small MLP (3 layers, 64--32--16, Softplus output) with squared-hinge penalty losses on density positivity, calendar monotonicity, and Roger-Lee bounds. \textbf{The key contribution is not the constraint formulation---which is textbook---but identifying and resolving the optimization pathologies that prevent it from working:} gradient domination from premature penalty application, scale mismatch between loss components, and initialization collapse into trivially flat solutions.

\textbf{Main result:} On S\&P 500 options with randomly dropped strikes (40--60\% missing), CINN achieves 22.7 bps RMSE at 40\% dropout with exploitable arbitrage below $5 \times 10^{-4}$ bps, while per-slice SVI produces 1.42 bps EPP, jointly-calibrated SSVI produces 0.87 bps EPP, and an unconstrained MLP with identical architecture produces 11.2 bps EPP. Adversarial off-grid evaluation (10,000 random points across 10 surfaces) confirms constraint enforcement generalizes beyond grid-aligned evaluation. Under full data, the accuracy gap versus unconstrained methods is 4.3\%. Downstream, constraint enforcement does not degrade hedging or market-making performance relative to unconstrained methods.

Constraint satisfaction is empirical, verified at evaluation grid points and adversarial off-grid samples. Calibration takes 412ms end-to-end per surface.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Volatility Surfaces, No-Arbitrage Constraints, Sparse Data Interpolation, Constraint-Informed Learning

\textbf{Acronyms:} SVI = Stochastic Volatility Inspired; SABR = Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho; MLP = Multi-Layer Perceptron; CINN = Constraint-Informed Neural Network\footnote{Not to be confused with Conditional Invertible Neural Networks, which share the same acronym in the generative modeling literature.}; EPP = Exploitable Profit Potential; RMSE = Root Mean Squared Error; bps = basis points (0.01\%).

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Problem: No-Arbitrage Under Sparsity}

Implied volatility surfaces must satisfy stringent no-arbitrage conditions---positive density, calendar monotonicity, finite moment bounds---to be consistent with any risk-neutral measure. Violating these conditions is not merely aesthetically displeasing; it creates exploitable trading opportunities and corrupts downstream Greeks.

The standard approach uses parametric models: SVI \citep{gatheral2004}, SSVI \citep{gatheral2014}, or SABR \citep{hagan2002}. These guarantee arbitrage-freeness by construction but suffer a critical weakness:

\begin{observation}[Per-Slice Parametric Brittleness]
For sparse strike grids ($\leq$8 strikes per expiry, corresponding to 60\% dropout from typical market data), standard \textit{per-slice} SVI calibration---fitting 5 parameters independently per expiry using Levenberg-Marquardt with random initialization---produces butterfly arbitrage opportunities averaging 3.2 bps EPP due to underdetermined parameter identification. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Wild extrapolations in the wings (insufficient curvature constraints)
    \item Spurious local optima from underdetermined parameters
    \item Sensitivity to noise that dwarfs the volatility signal
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Caveat:} Joint calibration methods mitigate but do not eliminate these pathologies. Our benchmark includes SSVI \citep{gatheral2014} with joint calibration across expiries; it still produces 2.14 bps EPP at 60\% dropout (Table~\ref{tab:sparse}). Further improvements may be possible with quasi-explicit SVI \citep{demarco2009} or no-arbitrage parameter constraints \citep{martini2022}. See Section~\ref{sec:related} for a full discussion.
\end{observation}

The alternative---neural network interpolation---handles irregular grids naturally but ignores arbitrage constraints entirely. Standard MLPs achieve excellent in-sample fit but produce surfaces where 30--40\% of grid points violate butterfly positivity.

\textbf{The core question:} Can we have both? Flexible interpolation under sparse data \textit{and} guaranteed arbitrage-freeness?

\subsection{Our Answer: Yes, With Caveats}

We show that constraint-informed neural networks can achieve both, with caveats we state upfront:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item We sacrifice 4--5\% fitting accuracy relative to unconstrained methods
    \item We require ${\sim}1.7\times$ more calibration time than unconstrained MLPs (412ms vs 245ms)
    \item We make no claims about optimality---our method finds \textit{a} feasible solution, not \textit{the} best one
\end{enumerate}

In exchange, we get:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Near-zero exploitable arbitrage ($<$0.05 bps) across all test conditions, including 50\% strike dropout
    \item Stable behavior under initialization variance (std of RMSE $<$ 0.8 bps across 20 seeds)
    \item No manual tuning per surface---a single penalty configuration works universally
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why This Matters}

\textbf{For practitioners:} Sparse grids are the norm, not the exception. Single-stock options, emerging markets, and illiquid tenors routinely have 5--10 strikes per expiry. A method that degrades gracefully under sparsity is practically essential.

\textbf{For researchers:} The failure mode of soft-penalty methods is underappreciated. We show that ``soft'' arbitrage penalties ($\lambda \leq 0.1$) achieve 90\%+ ``arbitrage-free'' points but leave 2--3 bps of exploitable profit---economically significant at institutional scale.

\textbf{What this paper demonstrates:} The no-arbitrage conditions and penalty methods are well-known individually. The contribution is showing \textit{why naive implementations fail}---gradient domination, penalty scaling, initialization collapse---and \textit{how to fix them}, producing a method that works stably under realistic sparsity where existing approaches do not.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Optimization pathology identification:} We identify gradient domination, penalty scaling failure, and initialization collapse as three non-obvious failure modes that prevent naive penalty-based constraint enforcement from working on volatility surfaces. We show how to resolve each (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

    \item \textbf{EPP metric:} We introduce \textit{Exploitable Profit Potential}, an economic arbitrage metric that exposes violations hidden by pointwise counts---SABR at 97.2\% ``arbitrage-free'' still has 0.83 bps exploitable profit (Section~\ref{sec:epp}).

    \item \textbf{Graceful degradation under sparsity:} We demonstrate that CINN maintains near-zero EPP under 60\% strike dropout where parametric models (including jointly-calibrated SSVI and eSSVI) produce material arbitrage (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}).

    \item \textbf{Downstream compatibility:} Hedging and market-making experiments show that constraint enforcement does not degrade downstream performance: CINN and unconstrained MLP produce statistically indistinguishable hedging quality, confirming that the accuracy cost of constraint enforcement is economically immaterial for risk applications (Section~\ref{sec:downstream}).

    \item \textbf{Adversarial arbitrage validation:} Off-grid adversarial sampling confirms constraint enforcement generalizes beyond evaluation grid points (Section~\ref{sec:epp}).

    \item \textbf{Open-source framework:} Full implementation with fixed-penalty and ALM training, structured sparsity dropout, baseline code, and EPP computation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Parametric Volatility Surface Models}

The SVI parameterization \citep{gatheral2004} provides a parsimonious 5-parameter fit per smile slice. Its arbitrage-free extension, SSVI \citep{gatheral2014}, jointly calibrates across expiries using a power-law ATM variance function, reducing underdetermination; eSSVI \citep{hendriks2019} extends this further with improved wing behavior. Robust calibration techniques include the quasi-explicit method of \citet{demarco2009}, which reduces SVI fitting to a constrained linear regression, and the no-arbitrage SVI parameter constraints of \citet{martini2022}, which guarantee butterfly-freeness by restricting the parameter domain. SABR \citep{hagan2002} provides dynamic smile models but does not inherently guarantee static arbitrage-freeness \citep{roper2010}. These methods excel under clean, dense data but face identification challenges under severe sparsity ($>$50\% missing strikes).

\subsection{Non-Parametric Constrained Surface Fitting}

\citet{fengler2009} introduced arbitrage-free smoothing of implied volatility surfaces using constrained least-squares in total variance space. \citet{andreasen2011} developed a non-parametric arbitrage-free interpolation method based on a one-step forward Dupire PDE, which achieves exact calibration with guaranteed no-arbitrage---a direct competitor to our approach that trades flexibility for hard guarantees. \citet{glaser2012} constructed arbitrage-free call price surfaces using locally constrained approximation with input data risk quantification. \citet{cousot2007} established necessary and sufficient conditions on discrete option prices for absence of static arbitrage, providing the theoretical foundation for grid-based no-arbitrage tests. \citet{davis2007} characterized the range of traded option prices consistent with an arbitrage-free model.

\subsection{Machine Learning Approaches to Volatility Surfaces}

Neural network methods for implied volatility have grown rapidly. \citet{ackerer2020} used soft penalty constraints in a neural network to smooth the IV surface while discouraging calendar and butterfly arbitrage. \citet{cohen2023} developed arbitrage-free neural-SDE market models with hard constraints on the network parameters. \citet{bergeron2022} applied variational autoencoders to generate synthetic volatility surfaces, and \citet{ning2023} extended this to arbitrage-free generation via SDE-based decoding. \citet{buehler2019} introduced deep hedging under market frictions, demonstrating neural networks' capacity to handle financial constraints.

An alternative paradigm calibrates a stochastic volatility model (rather than directly parameterizing the IV surface) and extracts the implied surface from simulated option prices. \citet{bayer2019} demonstrated deep calibration of rough volatility models, achieving fast parameter recovery via neural networks; \citet{horvath2021} extended this to deep learning-based volatility calibration with a focus on computational efficiency. These approaches inherit the no-arbitrage guarantees of the underlying model but are limited by model specification---if the assumed dynamics are wrong, the implied surface inherits the misspecification. Our direct IV parameterization avoids model choice but must enforce constraints explicitly.

For constraint-by-construction approaches, input convex neural networks \citep{amos2017} enforce convexity structurally and could in principle guarantee butterfly no-arbitrage in the strike dimension without penalty tuning. This represents a promising alternative to our penalty-based approach, trading flexibility for hard guarantees.

\subsection{Physics-Informed and Constrained Neural Networks}

Classical PINNs \citep{raissi2019} embed PDE \textit{equalities} in the loss. We apply the same penalty-augmented loss idea to financial \textit{inequality} constraints---a much simpler setting, since our constraints are pointwise inequalities rather than PDE residuals. The methodological parallels are instructive: our ``flat-surface collapse'' pathology (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is a domain-specific instance of the ``trivial solution'' failure mode documented in the PINN literature, where strong PDE residual penalties cause networks to learn $u(x,t) = 0$ rather than the true solution \citep{krishnapriyan2021}. Our warmup-then-ramp schedule mirrors the curriculum learning strategies that resolve this failure in PDE settings. \citet{wang2021} showed that gradient-based loss balancing---equalizing gradient magnitudes across loss components---can replace manual penalty weight tuning; our loss normalization to unit variance (Section~\ref{sec:method}) is a simpler variant of this approach. The PINN community has increasingly moved from soft penalties toward hard constraint methods: \citet{lu2021} showed that augmented Lagrangian methods outperform pure penalty approaches for PDE constraint enforcement, providing convergence guarantees that penalty methods lack. The augmented Lagrangian framework of \citet{basir2023} introduces adaptive dual variable updates, converting the multi-objective balancing problem into a principled constrained optimization. We implement both fixed penalty and ALM approaches in Section~\ref{sec:method}: the fixed penalty method is faster (412ms vs 680ms) and simpler to deploy, while ALM achieves slightly better accuracy (18.9 vs 19.4 bps RMSE) without requiring warmup scheduling. The tradeoff is between production simplicity and optimization principality; we document both and let the practitioner choose.

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations}

The Roger-Lee moment formula \citep{lee2004} establishes model-independent bounds on implied volatility at extreme strikes: $\limsup_{|k| \to \infty} \sigma^2(k) T / |k| \leq 2$. \citet{durrleman2004} derived tractable sufficient conditions for butterfly arbitrage-freeness, the ``Durrleman condition,'' which is equivalent to the density non-negativity condition we enforce. \citet{breeden1978} provided the foundational result linking option prices to risk-neutral densities. The non-convexity of the admissible surface set, which motivates our penalty approach over projection methods, is formalized in \citet{roper2010} and demonstrated by counterexample in Appendix~\ref{app:counterexample}.

\section{No-Arbitrage Conditions}
\label{sec:constraints}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Notation.} Throughout, $k = \log(K/F)$ denotes log-moneyness, $T$ is time-to-maturity, $w(k,T) = \sigma^2(k,T) \cdot T$ is total implied variance, and $g(k,T)$ is the Breeden-Litzenberger risk-neutral density. Subscripts denote partial derivatives: $w_k := \partial_k w$, $w_{kk} := \partial_{kk} w$.

We work in total implied variance $w(k, T) = \sigma^2(k, T) \cdot T$. We equip $C^2(\R \times \R_+)$ with the norm $\|f\|_{C^2} = \sup(|f| + |\partial_k f| + |\partial_{kk} f|)$.

\begin{definition}[Admissibility]
\label{def:admissibility}
A surface $\totalvar: \R \times \R_+ \to \R_+$ is \textit{admissible} if:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(A\arabic*)]
    \item \textbf{Positivity:} $\totalvar(\logstrike, \maturity) > 0$
    \item \textbf{Calendar:} $\partial_\maturity \totalvar(\logstrike, \maturity) \geq 0$ \quad (total variance increases with time)
    \item \textbf{Butterfly:} The Breeden-Litzenberger density $\density(\logstrike, \maturity) \geq 0$ where $\density$ is the denominator-free component of the risk-neutral density following \citet{gatheral2014}, Eq.~(7):
    \begin{equation}
        \density(\logstrike, \maturity) = \underbrace{\left(1 - \frac{\logstrike \, \partial_\logstrike \totalvar}{2\totalvar}\right)^2}_{\text{skew term}} - \underbrace{\frac{(\partial_\logstrike \totalvar)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\totalvar} + \frac{1}{4}\right)}_{\text{slope penalty}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial_{\logstrike\logstrike} \totalvar}{2}}_{\text{curvature}}
        \label{eq:density}
    \end{equation}
    The full risk-neutral density is $q(\logstrike) = \density(\logstrike) \cdot e^{-\logstrike/2} / \sqrt{2\pi \totalvar}$, so $\density \geq 0$ is necessary and sufficient for butterfly no-arbitrage. This is equivalent to the Durrleman condition \citep{durrleman2004}. Our penalty term penalizes violations of $\density \geq 0$ evaluated at discrete grid points.
    \textit{Interpretation:} Density positivity requires sufficient curvature to offset the slope penalty. Steep smiles need high convexity to remain arbitrage-free.
    \item \textbf{Roger-Lee:} $\limsup_{\abs{\logstrike} \to \infty} \totalvar(\logstrike, \maturity)/\abs{\logstrike} \leq 2$ \quad (moment explosion bound, \citet{lee2004})
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Non-Convexity]
\label{rem:nonconvex}
The admissible set $\mathcal{A}$ is \textbf{not convex}. The butterfly constraint couples $w$, $\partial_k w$, and $\partial_{kk} w$ nonlinearly. Intuitively, density positivity depends on both slope and curvature; averaging two admissible surfaces can produce insufficient curvature even when both originals have adequate curvature individually. Convex combinations of admissible smiles can produce negative density (see Appendix~\ref{app:counterexample}).

This means: (1) no projection operator exists, (2) standard convex optimization fails, (3) our penalty method is a heuristic, not a principled algorithm.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Efficient Constraint Evaluation}

\begin{proposition}[Local Criterion, following \citet{gatheral2014}]
\label{prop:local_criterion}
For $\totalvar > 0$ twice differentiable, define:
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda(\logstrike, \maturity) := \frac{\partial_{\logstrike\logstrike} \totalvar}{\totalvar} - \frac{(\partial_\logstrike \totalvar)^2}{2\totalvar^2} + \frac{\logstrike \, \partial_\logstrike \totalvar}{\totalvar^2}\left(1 - \frac{\logstrike \, \partial_\logstrike \totalvar}{4\totalvar}\right)
\end{equation}
Then $\density \geq 0$ iff $\Lambda \geq -1/(2\totalvar)$. This follows from algebraic manipulation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:density}; see \citet{gatheral2014}, Section~3 for the derivation. The criterion is computable via two autodiff backward passes.
\end{proposition}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Formal Problem Statement}

We state the volatility surface calibration problem as a constrained optimization over the network weights $\theta \in \R^d$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:constrained_problem}
\begin{aligned}
    \min_{\theta} \quad & \loss_{\text{MSE}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl( w_\theta(k_i, T_i) - w_i^{\text{mkt}} \bigr)^2 \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & g_j(\theta) \geq 0 \quad \forall\, j \in \mathcal{G} \quad \text{(butterfly density positivity)} \\
    & h_j(\theta) \geq 0 \quad \forall\, j \in \mathcal{H} \quad \text{(calendar monotonicity)} \\
    & w_\theta(k_j, T_j) / |k_j| \leq 2 \quad \forall\, j \in \mathcal{W} \quad \text{(Roger-Lee bounds)}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $g_j(\theta) = \density(k_j, T_j; \theta)$ is the Breeden-Litzenberger density (Eq.~\eqref{eq:density}) evaluated at grid point $j$, and $h_j(\theta) = \partial_T w_\theta(k_j, T_j)$ is the calendar monotonicity derivative. The constraint sets $\mathcal{G}$, $\mathcal{H}$, $\mathcal{W}$ are the evaluation grids for each constraint type.

The feasible set $\mathcal{A} = \{\theta : g_j(\theta) \geq 0, h_j(\theta) \geq 0\}$ is \textbf{not convex} (Remark~\ref{rem:nonconvex}), ruling out standard convex optimization and projection methods. We consider two approaches: fixed penalty (Section~\ref{sec:penalty}) and augmented Lagrangian (Section~\ref{sec:alm}).

\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{equation}
    w_\theta(k, T) = \text{Softplus}\left( f_\theta(k, \sqrt{T}) \right) \cdot 0.5 + 10^{-6}
\end{equation}

where $f_\theta$ is an MLP with layers $64 \to 32 \to 16$, GELU activations, and input-concatenated residual connections. The $\sqrt{T}$ scaling improves conditioning for short-dated options where $T \to 0$ would otherwise dominate gradients. The $0.5$ multiplier matches typical ATM total variance magnitudes ($w \approx 0.04$ for $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1\text{yr}$), improving optimization stability.

The penalty mechanism is therefore necessary, not redundant.

\subsection{Approach 1: Fixed Penalty Method}
\label{sec:penalty}

\subsubsection{Loss Function}

\begin{equation}
    \loss_{\text{total}} = \loss_{\text{MSE}} + \lambda_{\text{cal}} \loss_{\text{cal}} + \lambda_{\text{but}} \loss_{\text{but}} + \lambda_{\text{wing}} \loss_{\text{wing}}
\end{equation}

The penalty terms are squared hinge losses on constraint violations:
\begin{align}
    \loss_{\text{but}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\bigl(0, -g(k_i, T_i)\bigr)^2 \quad \text{(density positivity)} \\
    \loss_{\text{cal}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\bigl(0, -\partial_T w(k_i, T_i)\bigr)^2 \quad \text{(calendar monotonicity)} \\
    \loss_{\text{wing}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\bigl(0, w(k_i, T_i)/|k_i| - 2\bigr)^2 \quad \text{(Roger-Lee bound)}
\end{align}

\subsection{Training Stability: The Hard Part}

Training constrained neural networks reliably was harder than deriving the loss functions. This section documents why naive implementations fail and how to fix them.

\subsubsection{Gradient Domination}

Our first attempts used large $\lambda_{\text{but}}$ values from the start. This was a mistake. When butterfly penalty gradients dominate data-fit gradients early in training, the optimizer takes the path of least resistance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extremely flat surfaces (minimizing curvature trivially satisfies density positivity)
    \item Poor fit even at observed strikes (the network barely tries to match data)
    \item Slow or stalled convergence as competing objectives fight
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What worked:} A warmup schedule. We train for 100 epochs with $\lambda = 0$ (pure MSE), then ramp penalties linearly over 200 epochs. This lets the network first learn the gross term structure and smile shape---getting the ``skeleton'' right---before constraints enforce local regularity. We found that early constraint application caused the optimizer to collapse into trivially flat solutions that satisfied all constraints perfectly but fit nothing. The warmup avoids this trap.

\subsubsection{Penalty Scaling}

Another hard-won lesson: loss magnitudes matter enormously. The butterfly constraint has natural scale $O(1)$ while MSE has scale $O(10^{-4})$ in total variance units. We initially didn't realize this, and naively setting $\lambda_{\text{but}} = 1$ meant constraint gradients were $10^4{\times}$ larger than data-fit gradients. Training was dominated entirely by constraint satisfaction, with accuracy suffering badly.

\textbf{What worked:} Normalize each loss component to unit variance over a calibration batch before combining. After normalization, $\lambda$ values become interpretable: $\lambda = 1.0$ means equal priority between data fit and constraint satisfaction. This simple fix made hyperparameter tuning dramatically easier.

\subsubsection{Penalty Weights and Sensitivity}

After loss normalization, our production penalty weights are: $\lambda_{\text{but}} = 10.0$, $\lambda_{\text{cal}} = 5.0$, $\lambda_{\text{wing}} = 1.0$. The butterfly penalty is weighted highest because density violations are the most economically consequential. We train with the AdamW optimizer (learning rate $3 \times 10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size = full surface).

\subsubsection{Failure Cases}

Honesty requires documenting when this method fails:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme sparsity ($>$70\% missing):} At some point, there simply isn't enough data to constrain the surface. EPP rises to 0.5 bps and RMSE variance across seeds increases substantially.
    
    \item \textbf{Contradictory market quotes:} If the market IVs themselves imply arbitrage (e.g., crossed butterflies due to stale quotes), no admissible surface exists close to the data. The optimizer produces a compromise that satisfies neither objective well.
    
    \item \textbf{Insufficient warmup:} Skipping the warmup phase or ramping penalties too quickly causes the optimizer to find trivially flat solutions. We learned this the hard way.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Approach 2: Augmented Lagrangian Method}
\label{sec:alm}

The fixed penalty method requires manual $\lambda$ selection and provides no convergence guarantees. We implement the augmented Lagrangian method (ALM) as a principled alternative, following \citet{basir2023}.

\subsubsection{Formulation}

The ALM replaces fixed $\lambda$ with adaptive dual variables $\bm{\mu} = \{\mu_j\}_{j \in \mathcal{G}}$ and a single penalty parameter $\rho$. At outer iteration $k$, we minimize the augmented Lagrangian:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:alm}
    \loss_{\text{ALM}}(\theta; \bm{\mu}^k, \rho^k) = \loss_{\text{MSE}}(\theta) + \frac{\rho^k}{2} \sum_{j} \max\!\left(0,\; \frac{\mu_j^k}{\rho^k} - g_j(\theta)\right)^2
\end{equation}

This is the Powell-Hestenes-Rockafellar form for inequality constraints. After minimizing Eq.~\eqref{eq:alm} w.r.t.\ $\theta$ (inner loop, 50 epochs of AdamW), we update the dual variables:
\begin{equation}
    \mu_j^{k+1} = \max\!\left(0,\; \mu_j^k + \rho^k \cdot \max(0, -g_j(\theta^k))\right)
\end{equation}

If the total constraint violation does not decrease by at least 75\% per outer iteration, we increase $\rho^{k+1} = 2 \rho^k$ (capped at $\rho_{\max} = 1000$). This scheme requires no warmup heuristic: the dual variables naturally produce small penalties at points where constraints are satisfied and large penalties where they are violated.

\subsubsection{Convergence Behavior}

\begin{observation}[Empirical ALM Convergence]
\label{obs:convergence}
On 100 randomly sampled SPX surfaces (2008--2023), the ALM formulation (Eq.~\ref{eq:alm}) exhibits the following behavior. The sequence of constraint violations $\{V^k\}_{k=1}^K$ where $V^k = \sum_j \max(0, -g_j(\theta^k))$ satisfies:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item $V^k$ is monotonically non-increasing after a burn-in period of ${\sim}$3 outer iterations
    \item $V^K < \epsilon$ for $K \leq 10$ outer iterations with $\epsilon = 10^{-5}$, on 98 of 100 tested surfaces
    \item The dual variables $\bm{\mu}^k$ stabilize: $\|\bm{\mu}^{k+1} - \bm{\mu}^k\| < 10^{-4}$ at convergence
\end{enumerate}
\end{observation}

\textbf{This is an empirical observation, not a theorem.} In the convex setting, ALM convergence to a KKT point is guaranteed under standard regularity conditions (see \citet{bertsekas1999} for classical results). Our setting is non-convex (Remark~\ref{rem:nonconvex}), so these guarantees do not apply; the best available results for stochastic non-convex optimization \citep{ghadimi2013} guarantee convergence to approximate stationary points but not to KKT points of the constrained problem. Proving convergence would require establishing that a constraint qualification (LICQ or MFCQ) holds at the solution, which is non-trivial given the coupling between $w$, $\partial_k w$, and $\partial_{kk} w$ in the density condition. The 2\% of failures occur on days with contradictory market quotes (stale prices implying arbitrage in the raw data), where no admissible surface close to the data exists.

This would require establishing that the constraint qualification (LICQ or MFCQ) holds at the solution, which is non-trivial given the coupling between $w$, $\partial_k w$, and $\partial_{kk} w$ in the butterfly density condition.

\section{The Killer Experiment: Sparse Strike Stress Test}
\label{sec:sparse}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Standard benchmarks use full strike grids. Real markets are sparse. We systematically stress-test under dropout.

\subsection{Protocol}

\textbf{Data and splits.} We use SPX options from OptionMetrics IvyDB (January 2006 -- December 2023) with standard quality filters: moneyness $0.8 \leq K/S \leq 1.2$, days-to-expiry $7 \leq \text{DTE} \leq 730$, and bid-ask spread $< 50$ basis points in implied volatility terms. This yields approximately 800--1200 quotes per day after filtering. \textbf{Important:} CINN is calibrated \textit{per surface}---each daily surface is a separate training instance with no cross-day parameter sharing. There is no ``training set'' in the supervised learning sense; the model is fitted from scratch to each day's quotes. The 2006--2007 period was used solely for penalty weight selection ($\lambda_{\text{but}} = 10$, $\lambda_{\text{cal}} = 5$, $\lambda_{\text{wing}} = 1$); these weights were then frozen and applied unchanged to all 3,520 evaluation days (2008--2023). No evaluation-period data informed any hyperparameter choice.

\textbf{Baseline calibration details:} SVI is calibrated per-slice (5 parameters per expiry) using Levenberg-Marquardt with 10 random restarts. SSVI uses \textit{joint calibration} across expiries following \citet{gatheral2014}: the ATM total variance curve $\theta(T)$ is first fitted via cubic spline, then the SSVI parameters $(\rho, \varphi)$ are optimized globally across all slices simultaneously. SABR is calibrated per-slice with $\beta = 0.5$ fixed. Andreasen-Huge (AH) uses the one-step implicit finite-difference Dupire PDE method of \citet{andreasen2011}, which produces arbitrage-free local volatility surfaces by construction; we use the basic variant with piecewise-constant local volatilities and linear interpolation between observed strikes. Higher-order AH extensions (piecewise-linear or spline local vol) would narrow the RMSE gap but are not tested here. MLP and MLP-Soft use identical architecture to CINN (64--32--16, GELU, residual connections); MLP-Soft uses $\lambda_{\text{but}} = 0.1$ with no warmup. All neural models are trained for 300 epochs total.

For each daily surface:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Randomly drop $p\%$ of strikes uniformly at random
    \item Calibrate each model on remaining strikes
    \item Evaluate RMSE on held-out strikes and EPP on full grid
    \item Repeat 10 times per day, report mean $\pm$ std
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Key findings:}

    \item \textbf{Both per-slice and joint parametric models degrade under sparsity.} Per-slice SVI produces 3.21 bps EPP at 60\% dropout.     \item \textbf{Soft penalties scale poorly.} MLP-Soft (identical architecture to CINN but with $\lambda_{\text{but}} = 0.1$, no warmup) EPP triples from 2.1 to 6.4 bps as sparsity increases. The light penalty is insufficient to counteract interpolation artifacts.

    \item \textbf{Andreasen-Huge: hard guarantees at the cost of accuracy.} The AH method \citep{andreasen2011} is arbitrage-free by construction and maintains 0.00 bps EPP at all sparsity levels---a \textit{provable} guarantee that our penalty method cannot match. Its RMSE is higher: 44.7 bps at 60\% dropout vs 28.9 for CINN (55\% gap). \textbf{The honest question is whether a 35\% RMSE improvement is worth giving up hard guarantees for soft ones.} For desks pricing vanillas off the surface, the RMSE matters---a 16 bps fit improvement on a 30-delta put is economically significant. For desks feeding the surface into exotic pricers where any arbitrage contaminates downstream values, AH's hard guarantee may be worth the accuracy cost. Note: EPP values reported as $0.00$ in the table mean $< 5 \times 10^{-4}$ bps; the penalty method drives violations toward zero but does not enforce them exactly.

\subsection{Why Does Per-Slice SVI Fail?}

This deserves explanation because it's counterintuitive.

Per-slice SVI has 5 parameters per expiry. With 20 strikes, calibration is overdetermined and stable. With 8 strikes (60\% dropout from 20), calibration becomes barely determined, and:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Multiple local minima exist
    \item Optimizer may converge to wings-dominated solutions
    \item The ``arbitrage-free'' SVI parameterization only guarantees no-arbitrage \textit{if} parameters stay in the valid region; the optimizer can exit this region under noisy/sparse data
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Important qualification:} The per-slice failure mode is partially mitigated by joint calibration. Our SSVI benchmark uses joint calibration across expiries \citep{gatheral2014}, and indeed SSVI outperforms per-slice SVI at every sparsity level (Table~\ref{tab:sparse}). However, SSVI still produces 2.14 bps EPP at 60\% dropout---roughly $2/3$ of per-slice SVI's violations---because joint calibration constrains the ATM level but not the wing curvature under sparse data.

\textbf{Extended parametric baselines (synthetic data only).} We implement eSSVI \citep{hendriks2019} with power-law ATM term structure and Roger-Lee bound enforcement, calibrated via differential evolution with 10-restart L-BFGS-B refinement. \textbf{Important caveat:} eSSVI results are evaluated on \textit{synthetic} surfaces generated by our benchmark framework, not on the OptionMetrics data used for the main results (Table~\ref{tab:sparse}). This makes the eSSVI comparison non-parallel and should not be directly compared to Table~\ref{tab:sparse} values. On synthetic surfaces, eSSVI achieves 0.00 bps EPP (arbitrage-free by construction) with RMSE of 88--105 bps. The high RMSE reflects both the structural rigidity of the 5-parameter eSSVI form under sparsity \textit{and} potential calibration suboptimality---the results should be interpreted as an upper bound on eSSVI error. The key finding is that eSSVI maintains perfect no-arbitrage (0.00 EPP) at all sparsity levels, confirming that the ``parametric models break under sparsity'' framing in Section~\ref{sec:introduction} refers to accuracy degradation, not to arbitrage violations for globally-calibrated parametric forms. Quasi-explicit SVI \citep{demarco2009} with no-arbitrage parameter constraints \citep{martini2022} and piecewise-linear Andreasen-Huge remain untested and could narrow the RMSE gap further.

\subsection{Structured Sparsity Experiment}

To address the uniform dropout limitation, we implement a liquidity-weighted dropout model where the probability of a strike being missing depends on its distance from ATM and time to maturity:
\begin{equation}
    P(\text{drop} \mid k, T) \propto \exp(\alpha |k|) \cdot \exp(-\beta (T - T_{\min})) \cdot \bigl(1 - \gamma \exp(-|k|/0.05)\bigr)
\end{equation}
where $\alpha = 3$ controls wing steepness, $\beta = 2$ controls expiry decay, and $\gamma = 0.95$ protects ATM strikes. This produces sparsity patterns where ATM strikes survive with $>$95\% probability while deep OTM strikes are dropped $>$80\% of the time.

\subsection{Remaining Sparsity Limitations}

\textbf{Structured dropout is closer to reality, but still idealized.} Real-world sparsity includes pathologies our model does not capture: stale quotes that create spurious arbitrage in the raw data, wide bid-ask spreads that distort mid-quotes, and missing entire expiry slices (not just missing strikes within an expiry). The single-name equity results (Table~\ref{tab:single_name}) partially address organic sparsity, but AAPL, TSLA, and JPM are mega-cap names with clean quotes.

\textbf{Explicit scope limitation:} Based on our experiments, we recommend CINN for underlyings with $\geq$6 strikes per expiry and bid-ask spreads $<$50 bps in IV terms. Below 6 strikes, the constraint enforcement mechanism lacks sufficient grid resolution for density estimation, and the fallback rate (EPP $>$ 0.1 bps) rises above 10\%. For underlyings with $<$5 strikes or spreads $>$100 bps, we recommend Andreasen-Huge or SSVI with manual arbitrage correction. This scope restriction is conservative---the method may work beyond these bounds---but we have not validated it there.

\subsection{Wing Behavior and Roger-Lee Bounds}

The Roger-Lee bound (A4) requires $\totalvar(\logstrike, \maturity) / |\logstrike| \leq 2$ asymptotically. Our architecture uses $\text{Softplus}(\text{MLP}(\cdot)) \cdot 0.5$, which can in principle grow superlinearly in $|\logstrike|$ for out-of-distribution inputs. Within the training range ($|k| \leq 0.25$, corresponding to moneyness $0.8$--$1.2$), the wing penalty $\loss_{\text{wing}}$ enforces the bound at observed points. \textbf{Clarification:} Within this range, the wing penalty acts as a slope regularizer that discourages steep total variance growth, rather than enforcing the asymptotic Roger-Lee bound per se---the bound is meaningful only as $|k| \to \infty$, while our evaluation points satisfy $|k| \leq 0.25$. The penalty nonetheless serves its intended purpose: it prevents the network from learning superlinear wings that would violate the bound if extrapolated. For extrapolation beyond the training range, we do not guarantee Roger-Lee compliance. Production use should either (a) clip the surface at the moneyness boundary of the training data, or (b) blend with a parametric model (e.g., SVI) in the wings. We report results only within the training moneyness range.

\section{The EPP Metric}
\label{sec:epp}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Traditional arbitrage metrics count violating points. This misses economic significance.

\begin{definition}[EPP]
\emph{Exploitable Profit Potential} is the maximum risk-free profit from unit-notional butterfly/calendar spreads. A butterfly spread on three strikes $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ has payoff:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Butterfly}(S_T) = \max(S_T - K_1, 0) - 2\max(S_T - K_2, 0) + \max(S_T - K_3, 0)
\end{equation}
which is non-negative everywhere. If the cost (sum of call prices with the same signs) is negative, arbitrage exists. EPP is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{EPP} = \max_{\pi \in \Pi_{\text{butterfly}} \cup \Pi_{\text{calendar}}} \left\{ \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[\text{payoff}(\pi)] - \text{cost}(\pi) \right\}
\end{equation}
Computation is $O(N_K^3 \cdot N_T)$ per surface in the worst case, where $N_K$ is the number of strikes per expiry and $N_T$ is the number of expiries: butterfly evaluation requires iterating over all $\binom{N_K}{3}$ strike triples per slice, and calendar evaluation over all $N_K \cdot \binom{N_T}{2}$ strike-expiry pairs. In practice, restricting to adjacent-strike butterflies and consecutive-expiry calendars reduces this to $O(N_K \cdot N_T)$, which is the protocol we use. For a typical SPX surface ($N_K \approx 40$, $N_T \approx 12$), this takes $<$5ms.
\end{definition}

SABR at 97.2\% ``arbitrage-free'' still has 0.83 bps exploitable profit. At institutional scale (\$100M notional), that's \$8,300 per surface.

\textbf{Spread family coverage.} Our EPP metric tests butterfly and calendar spreads, which correspond to the density positivity (A3) and calendar monotonicity (A2) conditions respectively. We do not separately test risk-reversals or ratio spreads, but this is not a gap: risk-reversals (buy call, sell put at different strikes) are linear combinations of butterfly spreads and the underlying, so any risk-reversal arbitrage implies a butterfly violation that EPP would detect. More formally, conditions (A1)--(A4) are jointly necessary and sufficient for static arbitrage-freeness of European options \citep{cousot2007, davis2007}; since EPP directly tests (A2) and (A3), and our architecture enforces (A1) by construction (Softplus output) and (A4) via the wing penalty, the tested spread families cover the full arbitrage constraint set at evaluation grid points.

\textbf{Numerical precision note:} EPP is computed by exhaustive search over all strike triples (butterflies) and consecutive-expiry pairs (calendars) on the evaluation grid. CINN values reported as ``0.00'' mean $< 5 \times 10^{-4}$ bps---below the numerical precision of our search (grid spacing $\Delta k = 0.005$, $\Delta T = 7$ days). This is not provably zero; it means no exploitable arbitrage was found at the evaluation resolution. At 60\% dropout, the maximum single-trial EPP for CINN across all 3,520 days was 0.12 bps, observed on 2020-03-16 (COVID crash). Even this worst case is well below typical institutional transaction costs (0.5--2 bps for SPX options).

\textbf{Adversarial off-grid validation.} To test whether near-zero EPP is an artifact of grid-aligned evaluation, we perform adversarial arbitrage search: 10,000 random off-grid points per surface with density evaluation via finite differences, 5,000 random non-adjacent butterfly spreads per surface, and 5,000 random calendar spreads per surface. Across 10 CINN surfaces at 40\% dropout, all three tests produce 0\% violation rates and 0.0 bps maximum EPP. This confirms that penalty-based constraint enforcement produces smooth, arbitrage-free surfaces between grid points, not just at evaluation locations. We note that stochastic sampling is not an exhaustive search. To perform a stronger test, we conduct a gradient-based adversarial attack: for each surface, we initialize 200 random $(k, T)$ points in the training domain and run 100 steps of gradient descent on the density $g(k, T)$ (backpropagating through the full Breeden-Litzenberger computation to find the minimum). Across 10 surfaces, the minimum density found by gradient attack is 0.81---well above zero. No surface produces a negative density under adversarial optimization. This confirms that the penalty-based constraint enforcement produces genuinely smooth, positive-density surfaces, not surfaces that happen to be non-negative only at sampled points. The smoothness of the Softplus/GELU architecture limits the spatial frequency of potential violations, making gradient-based search effective.

\textbf{CINN has higher curvature variance than unconstrained MLP, not lower.} This is an expected cost of constraint enforcement, not a defect: density positivity (Eq.~\ref{eq:density}) requires that the curvature term $\partial_{kk} w / 2$ is large enough to offset the slope penalty, particularly in the wings where the smile steepens. CINN's 69$\times$ higher curvature variance relative to unconstrained MLP (Table~\ref{tab:greeks_stability}) raises a concrete concern: local vol surfaces extracted from CINN may exhibit greater numerical noise. We have not tested local vol extraction directly; this is an important direction for production validation. Desks using the surface for exotic pricing should evaluate Dupire stability empirically before adoption, or consider the AH baseline, which produces smoother second derivatives by construction.

\section{Discussion: Positioning and Practical Considerations}
\label{sec:discussion}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{CINN vs.\ SSVI in practice.} SSVI \citep{gatheral2014} remains the state-of-the-art parametric model under clean, dense data. CINN is not a replacement but a complement: use SSVI when data is clean and the strike grid is dense; use CINN when sparsity or noise degrades parametric identification. A promising hybrid: use SSVI as a prior, with CINN as a residual correction.

\textbf{Constraint-by-construction alternatives.} Input convex neural networks \citep{amos2017} could enforce butterfly no-arbitrage by construction, eliminating penalty tuning entirely. We chose penalties for flexibility---construction methods tend to underfit on complex smile shapes---but acknowledge this trades theoretical purity for practical convenience. Augmented Lagrangian methods \citep{lu2021, basir2023} offer convergence guarantees but add complexity; see Section~\ref{sec:related} for why we opted against them in the per-surface calibration setting.

\textbf{Per-surface vs.\ amortized inference.} CINN calibrates a fresh network per surface---300 epochs, 412ms. This is a per-instance constrained optimizer, not a learned volatility model. The design is deliberate: per-surface calibration eliminates carryover bias across regime changes (Table~\ref{tab:regime_change}), and the 412ms budget is acceptable for end-of-day risk. But the $12\times$ latency gap versus Andreasen-Huge (412ms vs 35ms) is a real cost, and the approach cannot scale to intraday recalibration of thousands of surfaces. The natural extension is amortized inference: training a single encoder network to predict constraint-satisfying surface parameters from sparse market quotes, eliminating per-surface optimization entirely. This reframes volatility modeling as learning a distribution over admissible surfaces rather than fitting individual ones. The penalty framework developed here transfers directly---the same loss function applies whether $\theta$ is optimized per-instance or predicted by an encoder. Meta-learned initialization, conditional neural processes, and neural operators are promising architectures for this extension.

\textbf{Production deployment.} For end-of-day risk reporting and illiquid options desks, CINN's 412ms latency is acceptable. For real-time market-making, it is not. Production use requires:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item A post-calibration arbitrage scan on a dense grid (${\sim}500$ strikes, ${\sim}$5ms overhead) to detect residual violations. We verified EPP grid convergence: halving the evaluation grid spacing from $\Delta k = 0.005$ to $\Delta k = 0.0025$ changes reported EPP by $< 0.002$ bps, confirming that discretization artifacts do not inflate our claims.
    \item A fallback model (SSVI) triggered when EPP exceeds 0.1 bps or when the warmup CV diagnostic (Section~\ref{sec:method}) signals a flat-surface failure. \textbf{Transition smoothness:} When the fallback triggers, we blend CINN and SSVI surfaces over a 3-day window ($\alpha_t = t/3$ for SSVI weight) to avoid discontinuities in Greeks. Empirically, the maximum delta jump at the CINN$\to$SSVI transition is 0.8\% of notional, compared to 2.1\% for an abrupt switch.
    \item Version-controlled model weights with automated monitoring. \textbf{Retraining triggers and priority logic:} (i) rolling 5-day mean EPP exceeds 0.05 bps, (ii) daily RMSE drift exceeds 15\% of historical mean, (iii) the butterfly violation rate on the dense scan exceeds 0.5\% of grid points. \textbf{When multiple triggers fire simultaneously} (e.g., during market stress), trigger (iii) takes priority (immediate recalibration with extended warmup), followed by (i) (recalibrate with current config), then (ii) (flag for manual review). If all three fire, the system falls back to SSVI and queues a full recalibration for the next quiet period.
    \item Day-over-day surface stability monitoring: across our test period, the mean overnight change in CINN total variance $\|\Delta w\|_\infty$ is 3.2 bps, comparable to SSVI (2.8 bps) and substantially more stable than unconstrained MLP (7.1 bps). Maximum overnight jump: 18.4 bps (2020-03-16).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We have shown that the tradeoff between flexible interpolation and empirical arbitrage-freeness can be closed under realistic sparsity: 4.3\% accuracy loss for near-zero exploitable arbitrage, verified at both grid-aligned and adversarial off-grid evaluation points.

\textbf{The core contribution} is identifying \textit{why} naive penalty-based constraint enforcement fails on volatility surfaces and \textit{how} to fix it. Gradient domination, penalty scaling mismatch, and initialization collapse are non-obvious failure modes that prevent straightforward application of well-known techniques. The solutions---warmup scheduling, loss normalization, and the resulting training protocol---are simple individually but jointly essential: skipping any one causes 10--40\% of seeds to converge to degenerate solutions (Table~\ref{tab:warmup_ablation}). We implement both fixed penalty and ALM approaches and document ALM convergence as an empirical observation (Observation~\ref{obs:convergence}).

\textbf{Main empirical findings:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under 60\% uniform strike dropout, CINN achieves 0.04 bps mean EPP (0.12 bps worst case) at evaluation grid points vs 3.21 bps for per-slice SVI, 2.14 bps for jointly-calibrated SSVI, and 0.00 bps for Andreasen-Huge (which pays a 55\% RMSE penalty for its hard guarantee)
    \item Soft penalties ($\lambda_{\text{but}} \leq 0.1$) are insufficient; they reduce but do not eliminate exploitable arbitrage. The Pareto frontier across 9 penalty configurations (Table~\ref{tab:lambda_sensitivity}) is smooth with no cliff-edge behavior
    \item Constrained training \textit{reduces} initialization variance (RMSE std 0.7 vs 2.1 bps), through capacity restriction rather than generalization-improving regularization
    \item The penalty mechanism, not the specific architecture or capacity restriction, is the primary driver of arbitrage-freeness: an unconstrained MLP with identical 2,961-parameter architecture produces 11.2 bps EPP at 40\% dropout (Table~\ref{tab:sparse}), while all architectures trained with the penalty achieve near-zero EPP (Table~\ref{tab:arch_ablation})
    \item The warmup-then-ramp schedule is essential: skipping warmup causes 40\% of seeds to converge to trivially flat solutions (Table~\ref{tab:warmup_ablation}). This pathology mirrors the ``trivial solution'' failure mode in physics-informed neural networks \citep{krishnapriyan2021}
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Scope and limitations.} Constraint satisfaction is empirical (verified at grid and off-grid points, not provable); the method is $1.7\times$ slower than unconstrained methods (412ms vs 245ms) and requires warmup scheduling. Real-world sparsity is structured---our structured dropout model approximates but does not fully capture market microstructure effects. Wing extrapolation beyond $|k| > 0.25$ is not guaranteed; the method is a per-instance optimizer, not a learned model (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Generalization beyond SPX and three mega-cap equities to FX, small-cap, or commodity surfaces is untested.

\textbf{When to use this:} End-of-day risk reporting under sparse grids, illiquid markets, regulatory requirements for arbitrage-freeness. \textbf{When not to:} Latency-critical applications ($<$100ms budget), clean high-frequency data where SSVI suffices, wing extrapolation without blending.

\textbf{Practical impact:} For SPX options under uniform sparsity conditions (40--60\% missing strikes), CINN reduces arbitrage to $<$0.05 bps at grid points while maintaining RMSE within 5\% of unconstrained MLPs and 35\% lower RMSE than Andreasen-Huge. On genuinely sparse single-name equities (AAPL, TSLA, JPM with 8--12 strikes/expiry), the same penalty configuration achieves near-zero EPP without retuning (Table~\ref{tab:single_name}).

\textbf{Future directions:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Amortized inference:} Replace per-surface optimization with an encoder that predicts constraint-satisfying parameters from sparse quotes in a single forward pass, enabling real-time calibration at scale (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
    \item \textbf{ALM convergence theory:} Prove the empirical convergence behavior documented in Observation~\ref{obs:convergence}---that ALM converges to a KKT point for the non-convex volatility surface problem under appropriate constraint qualification (LICQ or MFCQ).
    \item \textbf{Remaining parametric baselines:} Quasi-explicit SVI \citep{demarco2009} with no-arbitrage parameter constraints \citep{martini2022}; piecewise-linear Andreasen-Huge.
    \item \textbf{Constraint-by-construction comparison:} Quantitative benchmark against input convex networks \citep{amos2017} for hard butterfly guarantees.
    \item \textbf{Genuinely thin markets:} Small-cap equities (3--5 strikes, wide spreads), commodity options (seasonal gaps), EM FX.
\end{itemize}

\section{Counterexample: Non-Convexity}
\label{app:counterexample}

\begin{example}
Consider SVI smiles:
\begin{align}
    w_1(k) &= 0.04 + 0.1\left(-0.5k + \sqrt{k^2 + 0.01}\right) \\
    w_2(k) &= 0.04 + 0.3\left(0.5k + \sqrt{k^2 + 0.04}\right)
\end{align}
Both are admissible. Their average $w_{0.5} = 0.5 w_1 + 0.5 w_2$ has $g(k = 0.15) \approx -0.003 < 0$. Hence $\mathcal{A}$ is not convex.
\end{example}

\section{Why Not Projection?}
\label{app:projection}

A natural question: why not project onto the admissible set after training?

\textbf{Answer:} Because $\mathcal{A}$ is non-convex, no unique projection exists. Even if we could define one, computing $\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}(w)$ requires solving a constrained optimization problem \textit{per surface}---precisely what we're trying to avoid.

Some alternatives we considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iterative projection (ADMM-style):} Requires convexity for convergence guarantees.
    \item \textbf{Barrier methods:} Numerically unstable near constraint boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Architectures with built-in constraints:} E.g., integrating a positive function to guarantee monotonicity. These underfit in practice because they over-regularize.
\end{itemize}

Our penalty approach trades theoretical purity for practical stability.
