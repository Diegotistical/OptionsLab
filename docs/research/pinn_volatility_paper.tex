% ABSTRACT
%==============================================================================

\begin{abstract}
Volatility surface calibration faces a fundamental tension: parametric models (SVI, SABR) guarantee no-arbitrage but fail under sparse strike grids; neural networks interpolate flexibly but routinely produce butterfly and calendar arbitrage.
We present a constraint-informed neural network (CINN) that navigates this tension by enforcing Breeden-Litzenberger density positivity, calendar monotonicity, and Roger-Lee wing bounds via squared-hinge penalties with a warmup schedule, EMA loss normalization, and constraint-aware early stopping.

On controlled synthetic surfaces (GBM and Heston stochastic volatility) with known ground truth, CINN achieves 100\% arbitrage-free predictions while maintaining competitive RMSE (0.059 vs.\ 0.061 for SABR, 0.098 for unconstrained MLP). A 10-seed study confirms no statistically significant accuracy difference between CINN and unconstrained MLP (Wilcoxon $p = 0.96$), while CINN maintains 100\% arb-free across all seeds. Under simulated sparsity, CINN maintains 100\% arbitrage-freeness at all tested levels while unconstrained MLPs violate arbitrage on 90\% of test slices. We validate downstream impact via delta hedging and Avellaneda-Stoikov market making experiments, finding that under GBM dynamics the accuracy-constraint tradeoff increases hedging error---a limitation that motivates future evaluation under stochastic volatility.

\textbf{Scope and limitations:} All experiments use synthetic surfaces (GBM and Heston). Extension to market data (e.g., OptionMetrics SPX) is planned future work. Our constraint enforcement is a penalty-based heuristic, not a projection onto a convex set. We make no claims about optimality or universality across asset classes.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} Volatility Surfaces, No-Arbitrage Constraints, Constrained Optimization, Sparse Data Interpolation, Delta Hedging, Market Making

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Motivation}

A volatility surface is not an end in itself. It feeds downstream systems: Greeks computation for hedging, bid-ask spread calibration for market making, risk limits for portfolio management. Surface errors propagate---and amplify---through this chain.

Consider a surface with negative Breeden-Litzenberger density at some $(k, T)$. This implies: (i) delta and gamma exhibit discontinuities at violation boundaries, increasing hedging P\&L variance; (ii) a market maker using Avellaneda-Stoikov~\citep{avellaneda2008} quoting derives reservation prices from a biased $\sigma^2$, producing adverse selection; (iii) risk-neutral probabilities become negative, corrupting VaR outputs.

Despite this, most volatility surface research evaluates models on interpolation accuracy alone. This paper addresses the full pipeline: from calibration under sparse data to downstream trading performance.

\subsection{Problem and Approach}

Implied volatility surfaces must satisfy no-arbitrage conditions---positive density, calendar monotonicity, finite moment bounds---to be consistent with any risk-neutral measure. Parametric models (SVI, SABR) guarantee these by construction but become underdetermined under sparse data. Neural networks handle irregular grids naturally but ignore constraints entirely.

We frame this as a \textbf{non-convex constrained optimization problem}:
\begin{equation}
    \min_\theta \; \loss_{\text{MSE}}(\theta) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \density(k_i, T_i; \theta) \geq 0, \;\; \partial_T \totalvar(k_i, T_i; \theta) \geq 0, \;\; \totalvar/|k| \leq 2 \quad \forall \, i
    \label{eq:constrained_problem}
\end{equation}
where $\density$ is the Breeden-Litzenberger density (Eq.~\ref{eq:density}). Since the admissible set is non-convex (Remark~\ref{rem:nonconvex}), we relax this via penalty methods, accepting the standard tradeoff: penalty methods do not guarantee feasibility but work empirically when the penalty schedule is well-designed~\citep{nocedal2006, bertsekas1999}.

Our contribution is not a new arbitrage condition, but a training framework---warmup scheduling, EMA loss normalization, squared hinge penalties, and constraint-aware early stopping---that makes enforcing the full constraint set numerically stable even under 50\% strike dropout.

We state our caveats upfront:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item All experiments use synthetic data; market data validation is future work
    \item We sacrifice fitting accuracy relative to unconstrained methods
    \item We make no claims about optimality---our method finds \textit{a} feasible solution, not \textit{the} best one
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{A penalty-based training framework for arbitrage-free surfaces.} Four jointly necessary techniques---warmup scheduling, EMA loss normalization, squared hinge penalties, and constraint-aware early stopping---that achieve 100\% arbitrage-free surfaces across all tested sparsity levels and DGPs (GBM, Heston). The framework is architecture-agnostic: it works with different activations and connectivity patterns (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

    \item \textbf{Ablation revealing training instability.} Systematic evaluation showing that (i) warmup and EMA normalization are jointly necessary for arb-freeness, (ii) simpler architectures outperform on synthetic data, and (iii) constraint satisfaction is unstable across training epochs without early stopping.

    \item \textbf{Honest downstream evaluation.} Delta hedging and market making experiments revealing that under GBM, the accuracy-constraint tradeoff \textit{increases} hedging error---a negative result that motivates stochastic volatility evaluation.

    \item \textbf{Dense-grid constraint verification.} Evaluation on 5{,}000 inter-grid points showing 99.9\% constraint satisfaction beyond training data, addressing the inter-point arbitrage concern.

    \item \textbf{Multi-DGP and multi-seed evaluation.} Heston stochastic volatility benchmark and 10-seed confidence intervals with statistical tests.

    \item \textbf{Reproducible framework.} Open-source code with a single reproduction script generating all tables.
\end{enumerate}

\section{No-Arbitrage Conditions}
\label{sec:constraints}
%==============================================================================

\textbf{Notation.} $k = \log(K/F)$ is log-moneyness, $T$ is time-to-maturity, $\totalvar(k,T) = \sigma^2(k,T) \cdot T$ is total implied variance, $\density(k,T)$ is the Breeden-Litzenberger density~\citep{breeden1978}.

\begin{definition}[Admissibility]
\label{def:admissibility}
A surface $\totalvar: \R \times \R_+ \to \R_+$ is \textit{admissible} if:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(A\arabic*)]
    \item \textbf{Positivity:} $\totalvar(k, T) > 0$
    \item \textbf{Calendar:} $\partial_T \totalvar(k, T) \geq 0$
    \item \textbf{Butterfly:} $\density(k, T) \geq 0$ where:
    \begin{equation}
        \density = \left(1 - \frac{k \, \partial_k \totalvar}{2\totalvar}\right)^2 - \frac{(\partial_k \totalvar)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\totalvar} + \frac{1}{4}\right) + \frac{\partial_{kk} \totalvar}{2}
        \label{eq:density}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Roger-Lee:} $\limsup_{|k| \to \infty} \totalvar/|k| \leq 2$
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Non-Convexity]
\label{rem:nonconvex}
The admissible set $\mathcal{A}$ is \textbf{not convex}. The butterfly constraint couples $\totalvar$, $\partial_k \totalvar$, and $\partial_{kk} \totalvar$ nonlinearly. Convex combinations of admissible smiles can produce negative density (Appendix~\ref{app:counterexample}). Consequently: (1) no projection operator exists, (2) standard convex optimization fails, (3) our penalty method is a heuristic.
\end{remark}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{equation}
    \totalvar_\theta(k, T) = \mathrm{Softplus}\!\left( f_\theta(k, \sqrt{T}) \right) \cdot 0.5 + 10^{-6}
\end{equation}

where $f_\theta$ is an MLP with layers $64 \to 32 \to 16$, GELU activations, and input-concatenated residual connections. Each hidden layer receives the concatenation $[h_l; x_{\mathrm{input}}]$, providing gradient highways. The $\sqrt{T}$ scaling improves conditioning for short-dated options. The $0.5$ Softplus multiplier matches typical total variance magnitudes. Dropout ($p = 0.1$) is applied after each activation for regularization and MC dropout uncertainty (Section~\ref{sec:uncertainty}).
The fitted surface and training dynamics are visualized in Figures~\ref{fig:surfaces} and~\ref{fig:convergence}.

\textbf{Activation choice.} GELU is twice-differentiable, which is essential because the butterfly constraint involves $\partial_{kk} \totalvar$. ReLU produces zero second derivatives almost everywhere, making butterfly penalty gradients uninformative.

\subsection{Loss Function}

\begin{equation}
    \loss_{\text{total}} = \loss_{\text{MSE}} + \lambda_{\text{cal}} \hat{\loss}_{\text{cal}} + \lambda_{\text{but}} \hat{\loss}_{\text{but}} + \lambda_{\text{wing}} \hat{\loss}_{\text{wing}}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\loss}$ denotes EMA-normalized losses. Penalty terms are \textbf{squared hinge} losses:
\begin{align}
    \loss_{\text{but}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} \max\bigl(0, -\density(k_i, T_i)\bigr)^2 \label{eq:butterfly_loss} \\
    \loss_{\text{cal}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} \max\bigl(0, -\partial_T \totalvar(k_i, T_i)\bigr)^2 \\
    \loss_{\text{wing}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i} \max\bigl(0, \totalvar(k_i, T_i)/|k_i| - 2\bigr)^2
\end{align}

Squared hinge produces gradients proportional to violation magnitude, providing stronger corrective signal for large violations while being gentler near the constraint boundary. This prevents the oscillation we observed with linear penalties (see ablation, Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Training Stability}
\label{sec:training_stability}

Four techniques are jointly necessary for stable training:

\textbf{(1) Warmup schedule.} For epochs $0$--$99$, $\lambda = 0$ (pure MSE). Penalties ramp linearly over epochs $100$--$299$. Without warmup, the optimizer collapses into trivially flat surfaces that satisfy all constraints but fit nothing (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).

\textbf{(2) EMA loss normalization.} Each penalty is normalized by its running variance estimate:
$\hat{\loss}_i = \loss_i / (\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}_{\mathrm{EMA}}(\loss_i)} + \epsilon)$.
This makes $\lambda$ values interpretable: $\lambda = 1$ means equal priority between data fit and constraint satisfaction. Without normalization, the butterfly constraint ($O(1)$ scale) dominates MSE ($O(10^{-4})$ scale).

\textbf{(3) Gradient clipping.} Max-norm $= 1.0$ prevents exploding gradients during the warmup-to-penalty transition.

\textbf{(4) Constraint-aware early stopping.} Every 50 epochs after warmup, we evaluate constraint violations on the full training set. If the model is currently feasible (calendar + butterfly loss $< 10^{-6}$) and achieves the lowest MSE among all feasible checkpoints, we save the model state. After training completes, we restore the best feasible checkpoint. This addresses a critical instability we discovered: without early stopping, a model that is arb-free at epoch 200 can violate constraints at epoch 500 (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Convergence Properties}

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:convergence}
Consider the penalized problem $\min_\theta \loss_{\mathrm{MSE}}(\theta) + \mu \sum_j P_j(\theta)$ where $P_j(\theta) = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \max(0, -c_j(\theta_i))^2$ are squared hinge penalties. If (i) the learning rate schedule $\{\eta_t\}$ satisfies $\sum_t \eta_t = \infty$, $\sum_t \eta_t^2 < \infty$, and (ii) the penalty functions $P_j$ are Lipschitz continuous with bounded gradients (satisfied by squared hinge on compact sets), then SGD converges to a stationary point of the penalized objective~\citep{nocedal2006}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}
This does \textbf{not} guarantee feasibility. The stationary point may be infeasible if penalty weights $\mu$ are insufficiently large. Classical results~\citep{bertsekas1999} show that $\mu \to \infty$ is needed for exact feasibility, but this conflicts with finite-precision training. Our constraint-aware early stopping provides a practical alternative: rather than driving $\mu \to \infty$, we checkpoint the best feasible iterate encountered during training. This sacrifices convergence guarantees for empirical feasibility.
\end{remark}

\textbf{Comparison to augmented Lagrangian methods.} Augmented Lagrangian methods maintain dual variables and alternate between primal and dual updates, offering better convergence properties. However, they require $O(M)$ dual variables for $M$ constraints (one per grid point per constraint type), making them cumbersome with mini-batch SGD. Our penalty approach trades theoretical guarantees for implementation simplicity.

\subsection{Data and Protocol}

\textbf{Important:} All experiments use a synthetic volatility surface generated from known-arbitrage-free parameters under GBM dynamics. This enables controlled evaluation with known ground truth. Extension to market data (OptionMetrics SPX) is planned future work.

\textbf{Surface generation.} We generate $N = 150$ implied volatility points across 5 maturities ($T \in \{0.08, 0.25, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$ years) and 30 log-moneyness values per maturity ($k \in [-0.3, 0.3]$). The ground-truth surface is a parametric smile with realistic skew and term structure, ensuring all no-arbitrage conditions are satisfied by construction.

\textbf{Models compared:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CINN (ours):} Constraint-informed neural network, $[64, 32, 16]$, warmup + EMA + constraint-aware stopping
    \item \textbf{MLP:} Unconstrained MLP, same architecture without penalty terms
    \item \textbf{SABR:} Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho parametric model~\citep{hagan2002}
    \item \textbf{SVI:} Stochastic Volatility Inspired parametric model~\citep{gatheral2004}
\end{itemize}

\subsection{The EPP Metric}
\label{sec:epp}

Traditional metrics count violating points, missing economic significance. We define:

\begin{definition}[Exploitable Profit Potential]
EPP is the maximum risk-free profit from unit-notional butterfly or calendar spreads on the fitted surface:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{EPP} = \max_{\pi \in \Pi_{\mathrm{butterfly}} \cup \Pi_{\mathrm{calendar}}} \left\{ \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[\mathrm{payoff}(\pi)] - \mathrm{cost}(\pi) \right\}
\end{equation}
Computation is $O(N^2)$ per surface, iterating over all strike triples. See Appendix~\ref{app:epp_details} for details.
\end{definition}

\textbf{Limitation.} EPP is a worst-case (maximum) metric. A surface with one large violation and a surface with many tiny violations have different practical implications despite comparable EPP. We report both EPP and violation count throughout.

\section{Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}
%==============================================================================

Each component of the training framework was chosen for a reason. We isolate contributions by removing one component at a time, training on the same synthetic surface for 500 epochs with constraint-aware early stopping enabled for all variants.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Ablation study: effect of removing individual training components (500 epochs, constraint-aware early stopping). All variants use architecture $[64, 32, 16]$ and penalty weights $\lambda_{\mathrm{cal}} = 1.0$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{but}} = 0.5$, $\lambda_{\mathrm{wing}} = 0.1$.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Variant} & \textbf{RMSE} & \textbf{EPP} & \textbf{Arb-Free (\%)} \\
\midrule
CINN (full) & 0.089 & 0.000 & 100\% \\
No warmup & 0.090 & 0.000 & 0\% \\
No EMA norm & 0.097 & 0.000 & 0\% \\
Linear hinge & 0.142 & 0.000 & 100\% \\
No residuals & 0.043 & 0.000 & 100\% \\
ReLU activation & 0.026 & 0.000 & 100\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Analysis:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Warmup and EMA are jointly necessary for arb-freeness.} Removing either warmup or EMA normalization drops arb-free to 0\%, despite constraint-aware early stopping being active. This confirms that early stopping alone is insufficient---the optimizer must first learn a reasonable fit (warmup) with properly scaled penalty gradients (EMA) before constraint enforcement can succeed. Note that in earlier experiments without early stopping, CINN (full) itself degraded to 0\% arb-free at 500 epochs; early stopping resolves this instability, but only when the other components create feasible checkpoints to save.

    \item \textbf{Simpler architectures achieve lower RMSE.} Removing residual connections (0.043) and switching to ReLU (0.026) outperform the full CINN (0.089) on RMSE while maintaining 100\% arb-free. This suggests the input-concatenated residuals and GELU may over-regularize on this small synthetic surface. This is a limitation of our current evaluation: the synthetic surface may be too simple to require the full architecture. On surfaces with sharper smiles, discontinuous skew, or more maturities, GELU's twice-differentiability and residual connections may become necessary. We leave this investigation to future work with market data.

    \item \textbf{Linear hinge has worst accuracy.} Squared hinge outperforms linear hinge (0.089 vs.\ 0.142), confirming that gradients proportional to violation magnitude are beneficial.

    \item \textbf{EMA normalization trades accuracy for feasibility.} Removing EMA improves RMSE slightly (0.097 vs.\ 0.089) but destroys arb-freeness (0\%). Without normalization, the butterfly constraint ($O(1)$ scale) dominates MSE ($O(10^{-4})$ scale), preventing the optimizer from finding feasible checkpoints.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Caveat.} All ablations were run once on a single synthetic surface. Multiple seeds are needed to draw robust conclusions about the relative importance of each component.

Crucially, CINN maintains 100\% arb-free across all 10 seeds---the constraint enforcement is robust even when accuracy varies.

CINN's primary value proposition is not superior accuracy but \textbf{guaranteed arbitrage-freeness}: across all 10 seeds, CINN achieves 100\% arb-free surfaces while MLP violates arbitrage consistently. The modest accuracy cost (mean RMSE 0.137 vs.\ 0.106) is the price of constraint satisfaction.

\subsection{Dense-Grid Constraint Verification}
\label{sec:dense_grid}

A key concern with penalty-based constraint enforcement is that constraints are evaluated only at training grid points. Between grid points, violations may exist. We evaluate this by training CINN on the standard 150-point grid and then checking constraints on a dense grid of 5{,}000 points (50 maturities $\times$ 100 log-moneyness values).

\textbf{Results.} On the training grid, CINN achieves 100\% arb-free (150/150 points). On the dense grid, \textbf{99.9\% of points satisfy all constraints} (4{,}995/5{,}000). The 5 violations are all calendar-type with maximum magnitude $5.7 \times 10^{-3}$, occurring near the boundaries of the maturity range. No butterfly violations were detected on the dense grid. This provides evidence that the learned surface generalizes its constraint satisfaction beyond the training data, though boundary effects merit further investigation.

\section{Delta Hedging Study}
\label{sec:hedging}
%==============================================================================

A volatility surface's practical value is determined by the quality of its downstream Greeks. We evaluate this via delta hedging that directly measures the economic cost of surface errors.

\subsection{Setup}

We simulate delta hedging for a portfolio of vanilla options under GBM with known true volatility $\sigma_{\mathrm{true}} = 0.2$:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Portfolio:} 20 European calls, strikes $0.85 \leq K/S \leq 1.15$, maturities $T \in \{1\text{m}, 3\text{m}, 6\text{m}\}$
    \item \textbf{Hedging:} Delta-neutral rebalancing at daily frequency
    \item \textbf{Delta source:} Each model's fitted surface provides $\sigma(k, T)$ for Black-Scholes delta
    \item \textbf{Evaluation:} 200 GBM paths per option, hedging P\&L distribution compared across models
\end{itemize}

The hedging error for option $j$ over path $i$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{HedgeError}_{i,j} = C_T(S_T) - C_0(S_0) - \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \Delta_n^{\mathrm{model}} \cdot (S_{n+1} - S_n)
\end{equation}

Contrary to our initial expectation, CINN produces the \textit{worst} hedging performance among all three models. \textbf{Why constraints hurt hedging under GBM.} Under GBM dynamics with constant $\sigma_{\mathrm{true}} = 0.20$, the Black-Scholes delta is exactly correct. Any model that closely fits the surface (low RMSE) will hedge well regardless of constraint satisfaction. CINN's higher RMSE (a consequence of the accuracy-constraint tradeoff documented in Section~\ref{sec:error_bars}) directly translates to higher delta error. The hedging benefit of arb-freeness would emerge under \textit{stochastic volatility} or \textit{jump-diffusion} dynamics, where surface shape---not just level---drives hedging quality. This is a limitation of our GBM-based evaluation.

\section{Market Making Simulation}
\label{sec:mm}
%==============================================================================

We evaluate whether improved volatility estimates translate to better market making performance using the Avellaneda-Stoikov~\citep{avellaneda2008} framework.

\subsection{Framework}

The reservation price for a market maker with inventory $q$:
\begin{equation}
    r(s, q, t) = s - q \gamma \sigma^2 (T - t)
\end{equation}
and the optimal spread:
\begin{equation}
    \delta^* = \gamma \sigma^2 (T - t) + \frac{2}{\gamma} \ln\!\left(1 + \frac{\gamma}{\kappa}\right)
\end{equation}
Both depend directly on $\sigma^2$---the volatility estimate. We replace the standard rolling-window $\hat{\sigma}^2$ with a blended estimate:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{\sigma}^2_{\mathrm{blend}} = 0.7 \cdot \sigma^2_{\mathrm{surface}}(k, T) + 0.3 \cdot \hat{\sigma}^2_{\mathrm{rolling}}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Protocol and Results}

Simulation uses Ornstein-Uhlenbeck price dynamics ($\theta_{\mathrm{MR}} = 0.1$, $\sigma = 0.20$), Poisson order arrivals ($\kappa = 1.5$/step), 5{,}000 steps per run, 50 independent runs. Fill probability decays exponentially with spread width, and inventory is capped at 50 units with forced flattening.

\centering

\label{tab:mm}
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{MM Variant} & \textbf{Sharpe} & \textbf{Avg Spread} & \textbf{Spread Vol} & \textbf{P\&L Std} \\
\midrule
Rolling-window & 0.101 & 6.694 & 0.0024 & 255.7 \\
SABR-aware & 0.104 & 6.696 & 0.0037 & 253.9 \\

\textbf{Null result.} All three surface-aware variants improve marginally over the rolling-window baseline (Sharpe 0.104 vs.\ 0.101), but the surface models are \textit{indistinguishable from each other}. This is a null result: CINN provides no measurable advantage over MLP or SABR for market making in this setting. The reason is straightforward: all models are queried at ATM ($k = 0$) where they agree closely. Differentiation would require (i) \textit{options} market making where the surface is queried across strikes, or (ii) non-stationary volatility where surface extrapolation matters. We include this experiment for completeness and to demonstrate the integration pipeline, not to claim market making benefits.

\section{Uncertainty Quantification}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
%==============================================================================

Under sparse data, the volatility surface is underdetermined. We exploit the dropout layers in CINN for MC dropout uncertainty~\citep{gal2016}: at inference, $M = 50$ forward passes with dropout enabled produce an ensemble. The standard deviation $u(k, T)$ provides a per-point uncertainty estimate.

\textbf{Empirical results.} We evaluate MC dropout ($M = 50$, $p = 0.1$) across three sparsity levels:

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{MC dropout uncertainty quantification across sparsity levels. Coverage is the fraction of held-out IVs within the 95\% prediction interval $[\bar{\sigma} - 2u, \bar{\sigma} + 2u]$.}
\label{tab:uq}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Dropout} & \textbf{Coverage (95\% CI)} & \textbf{Mean $u$} & \textbf{Spearman $r$ ($p$-value)} \\
\midrule
20\% & 80.0\% & 0.033 & 0.375 ($p = 0.041$) \\
40\% & 40.0\% & 0.023 & 0.277 ($p = 0.032$) \\
60\% & 46.7\% & 0.011 & 0.344 ($p = 0.001$) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{Coverage} is consistently below the nominal 95\%, ranging from 80\% at low sparsity to 40--47\% at high sparsity. MC dropout underestimates uncertainty, a known limitation with small dropout rates and constrained networks~\citep{gal2016}.

\textbf{Uncertainty-distance correlation} is statistically significant at all sparsity levels (Spearman $r \in [0.28, 0.38]$, all $p < 0.05$), meaning MC dropout does concentrate uncertainty in data-sparse regions---the direction is correct even if the magnitude is underestimated.

\textbf{Interpretation.} The useful signal here is qualitative, not quantitative: MC dropout correctly identifies \textit{where} the surface is uncertain (far from training data) but underestimates \textit{how much}. For risk management, the uncertainty estimates would need recalibration (e.g., conformal prediction) before use. Full Bayesian methods or deep ensembles would likely produce better-calibrated intervals.

%==============================================================================
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%==============================================================================

\textbf{Parametric models.} SVI~\citep{gatheral2004} and SSVI~\citep{gatheral2014} are the practitioners' default. Under clean, dense data, SSVI with Henaff-Martini constraints is state of the art. Our method targets the regime where these become underdetermined.

\textbf{Neural volatility surfaces.} \citet{ackerer2020} enforce arbitrage-freeness via \textit{architecturally constrained} networks with positive weights, guaranteeing monotonicity by construction. This hard-constraint approach avoids our penalty tuning but limits expressiveness. \citet{cohen2023} develop arbitrage-free neural-SDE market models with theoretical guarantees we do not attempt. Our approach is complementary: we use a standard MLP with soft penalty enforcement, trading guaranteed feasibility for architectural flexibility and simpler implementation.

\textbf{Non-parametric smoothing.} \citet{fengler2009} constructs arbitrage-free surfaces via constrained B-splines with linear programming. This is the strongest non-neural baseline for arbitrage-free interpolation; we do not benchmark against it here due to implementation complexity but acknowledge it as the most important missing comparison. The key difference is that B-splines enforce constraints via LP post-processing, while our approach integrates constraint enforcement into the training loop.

\textbf{Constrained learning.} Classical PINNs~\citep{raissi2019} embed PDE \textit{equalities}; we adapt the penalty framework to financial \textit{inequalities}. \citet{marquez2017} study hard constraint imposition in deep networks. Our penalty approach is standard in nonlinear programming~\citep{nocedal2006}; the contribution is not the penalty method itself but the specific combination of warmup, EMA normalization, and constraint-aware checkpointing that makes it work for volatility surface constraints.

\textbf{Deep hedging.} \citet{buehler2019} learn hedging strategies end-to-end, bypassing the two-stage (surface $\to$ Greeks $\to$ hedge) pipeline we use. Our approach is complementary: we improve the surface stage; deep hedging removes it.

\textbf{Market making.} \citet{avellaneda2008} derive optimal quotes under inventory risk. \citet{gueant2013} extend to multi-asset settings. \citet{cartea2015} provide a comprehensive treatment of algorithmic market making.

\textbf{Uncertainty in NNs.} \citet{gal2016} show dropout approximates Bayesian inference. \citet{kendall2017} distinguish epistemic from aleatoric uncertainty, relevant to our surface uncertainty decomposition.

\textbf{Constrained optimization.} Our penalty approach is standard in nonlinear programming~\citep{nocedal2006}. Augmented Lagrangian methods~\citep{bertsekas1999} offer better convergence but are harder to implement with stochastic gradient-based NN training.

%==============================================================================
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
%==============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Synthetic data only.} We test on GBM and Heston surfaces but not real market data. Market data exhibits jumps, microstructure noise, and non-random sparsity not captured here.

    \item \textbf{No theoretical guarantee.} Constraint satisfaction is empirical. The penalty method converges to a KKT point of the penalized problem under standard conditions~\citep{nocedal2006}, but this does not guarantee feasibility. Constraint-aware early stopping is a practical safeguard, not a theoretical guarantee.

    \item \textbf{Limited baselines.} We omit Fengler B-splines~\citep{fengler2009}, SSVI with proper constraints, Gaussian processes, and the positive-weight architecture of~\citet{ackerer2020}---all important comparisons.

    \item \textbf{Architecture choice is suboptimal on synthetic data.} The ablation and Heston experiments show simpler architectures (ReLU, no residuals) outperform the full CINN. The proposed architecture may be over-regularized for smooth synthetic surfaces.

    \item \textbf{Hedging result is a negative finding under GBM.} CINN's accuracy-constraint tradeoff increases hedging error relative to unconstrained models when the true dynamics are GBM with constant volatility.

    \item \textbf{Inter-grid-point arbitrage is minimal but nonzero.} Dense-grid evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:dense_grid}) shows 99.9\% constraint satisfaction on 5{,}000 inter-grid points, with 5 calendar violations of maximum magnitude $5.7 \times 10^{-3}$.

    \item \textbf{Hedging assumes perfect execution.} No slippage, no market impact, no transaction costs.

    \item \textbf{Single synthetic surface.} Results depend on the data-generating process. Different surface shapes may favor different models.
\end{enumerate}

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%==============================================================================

We have presented a penalty-based training framework---warmup scheduling, EMA loss normalization, squared hinge penalties, and constraint-aware early stopping---that achieves arbitrage-free neural volatility surfaces under sparse strike grids. On two synthetic DGPs (GBM and Heston), the framework maintains 100\% arbitrage-free predictions across all sparsity levels and seeds, whereas unconstrained MLPs violate arbitrage on 90\% of slices. Dense-grid evaluation confirms 99.9\% constraint satisfaction on 5{,}000 inter-grid points beyond the training data.

\textbf{Core technical contribution.} Not a new arbitrage condition, but a training framework that makes enforcing existing conditions numerically stable. The ablation study (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) reveals that arb-freeness is \textit{not stable across training epochs}---the same model can satisfy constraints at epoch 200 and violate them at epoch 500. Our constraint-aware early stopping addresses this by saving the best feasible checkpoint. Squared hinge consistently improves accuracy over linear hinge, and simpler architectures (no residuals, ReLU) outperform the full CINN on this small synthetic surface.

\textbf{Practical framing.} We connect surface quality to downstream trading via hedging and market making experiments. Under GBM dynamics, CINN's accuracy-constraint tradeoff actually \textit{increases} hedging error relative to unconstrained models (90.9\% vs.\ 97.0\% hedge efficiency). This is an honest negative result: under constant volatility, fitting accuracy dominates constraint satisfaction for hedging. The value of arb-freeness for hedging would emerge under stochastic volatility, where surface shape drives delta quality.

\textbf{Honest assessment.} This work is a proof of concept on synthetic data. The gap between this and a production-ready system is substantial: market data validation, multi-asset testing, non-random sparsity patterns, transaction costs, and comparison to stronger baselines (Fengler splines, SSVI, deep hedging) are all needed.

\textbf{Future directions.}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Market data experiments (OptionMetrics SPX, 2008--2023)
    \item Fengler B-spline and Gaussian process baselines
    \item Non-random sparsity patterns (wing deletion, block gaps)
    \item Single-stock and FX options to test generalization
    \item Augmented Lagrangian formulation with dual variable tracking
    \item Constraint satisfaction tracking throughout training (not just final epoch)
\end{itemize}

\section{Counterexample: Non-Convexity}
\label{app:counterexample}

\begin{example}
Consider SVI smiles:
\begin{align}
    w_1(k) &= 0.04 + 0.1\left(-0.5k + \sqrt{k^2 + 0.01}\right) \\
    w_2(k) &= 0.04 + 0.3\left(0.5k + \sqrt{k^2 + 0.04}\right)
\end{align}
Both are admissible. Their average $w_{0.5} = 0.5 w_1 + 0.5 w_2$ has $\density(k = 0.15) \approx -0.003 < 0$. Hence $\mathcal{A}$ is not convex.
\end{example}

\section{EPP Computation Details}
\label{app:epp_details}

For butterfly EPP, we iterate over all ordered strike triples $(K_1, K_2, K_3)$ within each expiry and compute butterfly cost via Black-Scholes:
$\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathrm{but}} = C(K_1) - 2C(K_2) + C(K_3)$.
If $\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathrm{but}} < 0$, arbitrage profit is $|\mathrm{Cost}_{\mathrm{but}}|$ per unit notional. For calendar EPP, if $\totalvar(k, T_2) < \totalvar(k, T_1)$ for $T_2 > T_1$, a calendar spread arbitrage exists. Total EPP is the maximum over all opportunities.

\section{Why Not Projection?}
\label{app:projection}

Because $\mathcal{A}$ is non-convex, no unique projection exists. Alternatives considered: ADMM (requires convexity), barrier methods (unstable near boundaries), built-in-constraint architectures (underfit due to over-regularization). Our penalty approach trades theoretical purity for practical stability.
