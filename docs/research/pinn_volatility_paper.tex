\begin{abstract}
Volatility surface calibration under sparse strike data forces a tradeoff between interpolation accuracy and arbitrage-freeness. Parametric models (SVI, SSVI) guarantee no-arbitrage but suffer order-of-magnitude accuracy degradation when strikes are missing; neural networks interpolate flexibly but produce exploitable butterfly and calendar arbitrage.

We show that penalty-based constraint enforcement---squared-hinge losses on density positivity, calendar monotonicity, and Roger-Lee bounds---combined with specific optimization techniques (warmup scheduling, loss normalization) \textbf{achieves the best accuracy--arbitrage tradeoff under sparsity among tested methods}. The key contribution is not the constraint formulation, which is textbook, but identifying and resolving the optimization pathologies that prevent it from working: gradient domination, scale mismatch, and initialization collapse.

Under 40--60\% strike dropout on synthetic equity surfaces, CINN achieves near-zero exploitable arbitrage ($<$0.05 bps EPP) while maintaining RMSE within 5\% of unconstrained methods and 35--55\% lower than the nearest arbitrage-free competitor (Andreasen-Huge). Constraint satisfaction is empirical, not provable---verified at grid points and via gradient-based adversarial search, but without formal guarantees.
\end{abstract}

\textbf{Keywords:} Volatility Surfaces, No-Arbitrage Constraints, Sparse Data Interpolation, Constraint-Informed Learning

\textbf{Acronyms:} SVI = Stochastic Volatility Inspired; SABR = Stochastic Alpha Beta Rho; MLP = Multi-Layer Perceptron; CINN = Constraint-Informed Neural Network\footnote{Not to be confused with Conditional Invertible Neural Networks, which share the same acronym in the generative modeling literature.}; EPP = Exploitable Profit Potential; RMSE = Root Mean Squared Error; bps = basis points (0.01\%).

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{The Problem: No-Arbitrage Under Sparsity}

Implied volatility surfaces must satisfy stringent no-arbitrage conditions---positive density, calendar monotonicity, finite moment bounds---to be consistent with any risk-neutral measure. Violating these conditions is not merely aesthetically displeasing; it creates exploitable trading opportunities and corrupts downstream Greeks.

The standard approach uses parametric models: SVI \citep{gatheral2004}, SSVI \citep{gatheral2014}, or SABR \citep{hagan2002}. These guarantee arbitrage-freeness by construction but suffer a critical weakness:

\begin{observation}[Per-Slice Parametric Brittleness]
For sparse strike grids ($\leq$8 strikes per expiry, corresponding to 60\% dropout from typical market data), standard \textit{per-slice} SVI calibration---fitting 5 parameters independently per expiry using Levenberg-Marquardt with random initialization---produces butterfly arbitrage opportunities averaging 3.2 bps EPP due to underdetermined parameter identification. Specifically:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*)]
    \item Wild extrapolations in the wings (insufficient curvature constraints)
    \item Spurious local optima from underdetermined parameters
    \item Sensitivity to noise that dwarfs the volatility signal
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Caveat:} Joint calibration methods mitigate but do not eliminate these pathologies. Our benchmark includes SSVI \citep{gatheral2014} with joint calibration across expiries; it still produces 2.14 bps EPP at 60\% dropout (Table~\ref{tab:sparse}). Further improvements may be possible with quasi-explicit SVI \citep{demarco2009} or no-arbitrage parameter constraints \citep{martini2022}. See Section~\ref{sec:related} for a full discussion.
\end{observation}

The alternative---neural network interpolation---handles irregular grids naturally but ignores arbitrage constraints entirely. Standard MLPs achieve excellent in-sample fit but produce surfaces where 30--40\% of grid points violate butterfly positivity.

\textbf{The core question:} Can we systematically achieve the best accuracy--arbitrage tradeoff under sparse data?

\subsection{Our Answer: Penalty-Based Constraint Enforcement}

We show that constraint-informed neural networks achieve the best accuracy--arbitrage tradeoff under sparsity among tested methods, though at a latency cost relative to hard-guarantee alternatives (412ms vs 42ms for Andreasen-Huge). The tradeoff costs are:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item 4--5\% fitting accuracy relative to unconstrained methods (the price of constraint satisfaction)
    \item ${\sim}1.7\times$ more calibration time than unconstrained MLPs (412ms vs 245ms)
    \item Empirical, not provable, constraint satisfaction
\end{enumerate}

The gains:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Near-zero exploitable arbitrage ($<$0.05 bps EPP) across all tested sparsity levels, below typical transaction costs
    \item Stable convergence across initializations (RMSE std $<$ 0.8 bps across 20 seeds)
    \item Universal penalty configuration---no per-surface tuning
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why This Matters}

\textbf{For practitioners:} Sparse grids are the norm, not the exception. Single-stock options, emerging markets, and illiquid tenors routinely have 5--10 strikes per expiry. A method that degrades gracefully under sparsity is practically essential.

\textbf{For researchers:} The failure mode of soft-penalty methods is underappreciated. We show that ``soft'' arbitrage penalties ($\lambda \leq 0.1$) achieve 90\%+ ``arbitrage-free'' points but leave 2--3 bps of exploitable profit---economically significant at institutional scale.

\textbf{What this paper demonstrates:} The no-arbitrage conditions and penalty methods are well-known individually. The contribution is showing \textit{why naive implementations fail}---gradient domination, penalty scaling, initialization collapse---and \textit{how to fix them}, producing a method that works stably under realistic sparsity where existing approaches do not.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Optimization pathology identification:} We identify gradient domination, penalty scaling failure, and initialization collapse as three non-obvious failure modes that prevent naive penalty-based constraint enforcement from working on volatility surfaces. We show how to resolve each (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

    \item \textbf{EPP metric:} We introduce \textit{Exploitable Profit Potential}, an economic arbitrage metric that exposes violations hidden by pointwise counts---SABR at 97.2\% ``arbitrage-free'' still has 0.83 bps exploitable profit (Section~\ref{sec:epp}).

    \item \textbf{Graceful degradation under sparsity:} We demonstrate that CINN maintains near-zero EPP under 60\% strike dropout where parametric models (including jointly-calibrated SSVI and eSSVI) produce material arbitrage (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}).

    \item \textbf{Downstream compatibility:} Preliminary hedging experiments show that constraint enforcement does not degrade downstream performance: CINN and unconstrained MLP produce statistically indistinguishable hedging quality (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

    \item \textbf{Adversarial arbitrage validation:} Off-grid adversarial sampling confirms constraint enforcement generalizes beyond evaluation grid points (Section~\ref{sec:epp}).

    \item \textbf{Negative result---ICNN:} We show that input convex neural networks, despite providing architectural convexity guarantees, fail to fit realistic smiles under proper Gatheral density enforcement (${\sim}$630 bps RMSE), confirming that constraint-by-construction is not viable for volatility surfaces (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}).

    \item \textbf{Open-source framework:} Full implementation with fixed-penalty and ALM training, structured sparsity dropout, baseline code, and EPP computation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Parametric Volatility Surface Models}

The SVI parameterization \citep{gatheral2004} provides a parsimonious 5-parameter fit per smile slice. Its arbitrage-free extension, SSVI \citep{gatheral2014}, jointly calibrates across expiries using a power-law ATM variance function, reducing underdetermination; eSSVI \citep{hendriks2019} extends this further with improved wing behavior. Robust calibration techniques include the quasi-explicit method of \citet{demarco2009}, which reduces SVI fitting to a constrained linear regression, and the no-arbitrage SVI parameter constraints of \citet{martini2022}, which guarantee butterfly-freeness by restricting the parameter domain. SABR \citep{hagan2002} provides dynamic smile models but does not inherently guarantee static arbitrage-freeness \citep{roper2010}. These methods excel under clean, dense data but face identification challenges under severe sparsity ($>$50\% missing strikes).

\subsection{Non-Parametric Constrained Surface Fitting}

\citet{fengler2009} introduced arbitrage-free smoothing of implied volatility surfaces using constrained least-squares in total variance space. \citet{andreasen2011} developed a non-parametric arbitrage-free interpolation method based on a one-step forward Dupire PDE, which achieves exact calibration with guaranteed no-arbitrage---a direct competitor to our approach that trades flexibility for hard guarantees. \citet{glaser2012} constructed arbitrage-free call price surfaces using locally constrained approximation with input data risk quantification. \citet{cousot2007} established necessary and sufficient conditions on discrete option prices for absence of static arbitrage, providing the theoretical foundation for grid-based no-arbitrage tests. \citet{davis2007} characterized the range of traded option prices consistent with an arbitrage-free model.

\subsection{Machine Learning Approaches to Volatility Surfaces}

Neural network methods for implied volatility have grown rapidly. \citet{ackerer2020} used soft penalty constraints in a neural network to smooth the IV surface while discouraging calendar and butterfly arbitrage. \citet{cohen2023} developed arbitrage-free neural-SDE market models with hard constraints on the network parameters. \citet{bergeron2022} applied variational autoencoders to generate synthetic volatility surfaces, and \citet{ning2023} extended this to arbitrage-free generation via SDE-based decoding. \citet{buehler2019} introduced deep hedging under market frictions, demonstrating neural networks' capacity to handle financial constraints.

An alternative paradigm calibrates a stochastic volatility model (rather than directly parameterizing the IV surface) and extracts the implied surface from simulated option prices. \citet{bayer2019} demonstrated deep calibration of rough volatility models, achieving fast parameter recovery via neural networks; \citet{horvath2021} extended this to deep learning-based volatility calibration with a focus on computational efficiency. These approaches inherit the no-arbitrage guarantees of the underlying model but are limited by model specification---if the assumed dynamics are wrong, the implied surface inherits the misspecification. Our direct IV parameterization avoids model choice but must enforce constraints explicitly.

For constraint-by-construction approaches, input convex neural networks \citep{amos2017} enforce convexity structurally via non-negative passthrough weights. However, convexity of total variance $w(k)$ in log-moneyness is \textit{not} equivalent to the true butterfly no-arbitrage condition, which requires the Gatheral density $g(k) \geq 0$ \citep{gatheral2004}. We evaluate ICNN as a baseline and find that the non-negative weight constraints are too rigid to fit realistic smiles under proper Gatheral enforcement (Section~\ref{sec:sparse}).

\subsection{Physics-Informed and Constrained Neural Networks}

Classical PINNs \citep{raissi2019} embed PDE equalities in the loss; we apply the same idea to financial inequality constraints. Our ``flat-surface collapse'' pathology (Section~\ref{sec:method}) mirrors the ``trivial solution'' failure in PINNs \citep{krishnapriyan2021}, and our warmup schedule mirrors curriculum learning strategies from that literature. Our loss normalization is a simplified variant of the gradient-based balancing of \citet{wang2021}. We implement both fixed penalty and augmented Lagrangian approaches following \citet{lu2021, basir2023}; the tradeoff is production simplicity vs.\ optimization principality (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\subsection{Theoretical Foundations}

The Roger-Lee moment formula \citep{lee2004}, Durrleman's density positivity condition \citep{durrleman2004}, and the Breeden-Litzenberger pricing-density link \citep{breeden1978} provide the theoretical foundation for our constraint set. The non-convexity of the admissible surface set \citep{roper2010} motivates penalty over projection methods (Appendix~\ref{app:counterexample}).

\section{No-Arbitrage Conditions}
\label{sec:constraints}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Notation.} Throughout, $k = \log(K/F)$ denotes log-moneyness, $T$ is time-to-maturity, $w(k,T) = \sigma^2(k,T) \cdot T$ is total implied variance, and $g(k,T)$ is the Breeden-Litzenberger risk-neutral density. Subscripts denote partial derivatives: $w_k := \partial_k w$, $w_{kk} := \partial_{kk} w$.

We work in total implied variance $w(k, T) = \sigma^2(k, T) \cdot T$. We equip $C^2(\R \times \R_+)$ with the norm $\|f\|_{C^2} = \sup(|f| + |\partial_k f| + |\partial_{kk} f|)$.

\begin{definition}[Admissibility]
\label{def:admissibility}
A surface $\totalvar: \R \times \R_+ \to \R_+$ is \textit{admissible} if:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(A\arabic*)]
    \item \textbf{Positivity:} $\totalvar(\logstrike, \maturity) > 0$
    \item \textbf{Calendar:} $\partial_\maturity \totalvar(\logstrike, \maturity) \geq 0$ \quad (total variance increases with time)
    \item \textbf{Butterfly:} The Breeden-Litzenberger density $\density(\logstrike, \maturity) \geq 0$ where $\density$ is the denominator-free component of the risk-neutral density following \citet{gatheral2014}, Eq.~(7):
    \begin{equation}
        \density(\logstrike, \maturity) = \underbrace{\left(1 - \frac{\logstrike \, \partial_\logstrike \totalvar}{2\totalvar}\right)^2}_{\text{skew term}} - \underbrace{\frac{(\partial_\logstrike \totalvar)^2}{4}\left(\frac{1}{\totalvar} + \frac{1}{4}\right)}_{\text{slope penalty}} + \underbrace{\frac{\partial_{\logstrike\logstrike} \totalvar}{2}}_{\text{curvature}}
        \label{eq:density}
    \end{equation}
    The full risk-neutral density is $q(\logstrike) = \density(\logstrike) \cdot e^{-\logstrike/2} / \sqrt{2\pi \totalvar}$, so $\density \geq 0$ is necessary and sufficient for butterfly no-arbitrage. This is equivalent to the Durrleman condition \citep{durrleman2004}. Our penalty term penalizes violations of $\density \geq 0$ evaluated at discrete grid points.
    \textit{Interpretation:} Density positivity requires sufficient curvature to offset the slope penalty. Steep smiles need high convexity to remain arbitrage-free.
    \item \textbf{Roger-Lee:} $\limsup_{\abs{\logstrike} \to \infty} \totalvar(\logstrike, \maturity)/\abs{\logstrike} \leq 2$ \quad (moment explosion bound, \citet{lee2004})
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Non-Convexity]
\label{rem:nonconvex}
The admissible set $\mathcal{A}$ is \textbf{not convex}. The butterfly constraint couples $w$, $\partial_k w$, and $\partial_{kk} w$ nonlinearly. Intuitively, density positivity depends on both slope and curvature; averaging two admissible surfaces can produce insufficient curvature even when both originals have adequate curvature individually. Convex combinations of admissible smiles can produce negative density (see Appendix~\ref{app:counterexample}).

This means: (1) no projection operator exists, (2) standard convex optimization fails, (3) our penalty method is a heuristic, not a principled algorithm.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Efficient Constraint Evaluation}

\begin{proposition}[Local Criterion, following \citet{gatheral2014}]
\label{prop:local_criterion}
For $\totalvar > 0$ twice differentiable, define:
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda(\logstrike, \maturity) := \frac{\partial_{\logstrike\logstrike} \totalvar}{\totalvar} - \frac{(\partial_\logstrike \totalvar)^2}{2\totalvar^2} + \frac{\logstrike \, \partial_\logstrike \totalvar}{\totalvar^2}\left(1 - \frac{\logstrike \, \partial_\logstrike \totalvar}{4\totalvar}\right)
\end{equation}
Then $\density \geq 0$ iff $\Lambda \geq -1/(2\totalvar)$. This follows from algebraic manipulation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:density}; see \citet{gatheral2014}, Section~3 for the derivation. The criterion is computable via two autodiff backward passes.
\end{proposition}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\subsection{Formal Problem Statement}

We state the volatility surface calibration problem as a constrained optimization over the network weights $\theta \in \R^d$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:constrained_problem}
\begin{aligned}
    \min_{\theta} \quad & \loss_{\text{MSE}}(\theta) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \bigl( w_\theta(k_i, T_i) - w_i^{\text{mkt}} \bigr)^2 \\
    \text{s.t.} \quad & g_j(\theta) \geq 0 \quad \forall\, j \in \mathcal{G} \quad \text{(butterfly density positivity)} \\
    & h_j(\theta) \geq 0 \quad \forall\, j \in \mathcal{H} \quad \text{(calendar monotonicity)} \\
    & w_\theta(k_j, T_j) / |k_j| \leq 2 \quad \forall\, j \in \mathcal{W} \quad \text{(Roger-Lee bounds)}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

where $g_j(\theta) = \density(k_j, T_j; \theta)$ is the Breeden-Litzenberger density (Eq.~\eqref{eq:density}) evaluated at grid point $j$, and $h_j(\theta) = \partial_T w_\theta(k_j, T_j)$ is the calendar monotonicity derivative. The constraint sets $\mathcal{G}$, $\mathcal{H}$, $\mathcal{W}$ are the evaluation grids for each constraint type.

The feasible set $\mathcal{A} = \{\theta : g_j(\theta) \geq 0, h_j(\theta) \geq 0\}$ is \textbf{not convex} (Remark~\ref{rem:nonconvex}), ruling out standard convex optimization and projection methods. We consider two approaches: fixed penalty (Section~\ref{sec:penalty}) and augmented Lagrangian (Section~\ref{sec:alm}).

\subsection{Architecture}

\begin{equation}
    w_\theta(k, T) = \text{Softplus}\left( f_\theta(k, \sqrt{T}) \right) \cdot 0.5 + 10^{-6}
\end{equation}

where $f_\theta$ is an MLP with layers $64 \to 32 \to 16$, GELU activations, and input-concatenated residual connections. The $\sqrt{T}$ scaling improves conditioning for short-dated options where $T \to 0$ would otherwise dominate gradients. The $0.5$ multiplier matches typical ATM total variance magnitudes ($w \approx 0.04$ for $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1\text{yr}$), improving optimization stability.

The penalty mechanism is therefore necessary, not redundant.

\subsection{Approach 1: Fixed Penalty Method}
\label{sec:penalty}

\subsubsection{Loss Function}

\begin{equation}
    \loss_{\text{total}} = \loss_{\text{MSE}} + \lambda_{\text{cal}} \loss_{\text{cal}} + \lambda_{\text{but}} \loss_{\text{but}} + \lambda_{\text{wing}} \loss_{\text{wing}}
\end{equation}

The penalty terms are squared hinge losses on constraint violations:
\begin{align}
    \loss_{\text{but}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\bigl(0, -g(k_i, T_i)\bigr)^2 \quad \text{(density positivity)} \\
    \loss_{\text{cal}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\bigl(0, -\partial_T w(k_i, T_i)\bigr)^2 \quad \text{(calendar monotonicity)} \\
    \loss_{\text{wing}} &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \max\bigl(0, w(k_i, T_i)/|k_i| - 2\bigr)^2 \quad \text{(Roger-Lee bound)}
\end{align}

\subsection{Training Stability: The Hard Part}

Training constrained neural networks reliably was harder than deriving the loss functions. This section documents why naive implementations fail and how to fix them.

\subsubsection{Gradient Domination}

Our first attempts used large $\lambda_{\text{but}}$ values from the start. This was a mistake. When butterfly penalty gradients dominate data-fit gradients early in training, the optimizer takes the path of least resistance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Extremely flat surfaces (minimizing curvature trivially satisfies density positivity)
    \item Poor fit even at observed strikes (the network barely tries to match data)
    \item Slow or stalled convergence as competing objectives fight
\end{itemize}

\textbf{What worked:} A warmup schedule. We train for 100 epochs with $\lambda = 0$ (pure MSE), then ramp penalties linearly over 200 epochs. This lets the network first learn the gross term structure and smile shape---getting the ``skeleton'' right---before constraints enforce local regularity. We found that early constraint application caused the optimizer to collapse into trivially flat solutions that satisfied all constraints perfectly but fit nothing. The warmup avoids this trap.

\subsubsection{Penalty Scaling}

Another hard-won lesson: loss magnitudes matter enormously. The butterfly constraint has natural scale $O(1)$ while MSE has scale $O(10^{-4})$ in total variance units. We initially didn't realize this, and naively setting $\lambda_{\text{but}} = 1$ meant constraint gradients were $10^4{\times}$ larger than data-fit gradients. Training was dominated entirely by constraint satisfaction, with accuracy suffering badly.

\textbf{What worked:} Normalize each loss component to unit variance over a calibration batch before combining. After normalization, $\lambda$ values become interpretable: $\lambda = 1.0$ means equal priority between data fit and constraint satisfaction. This simple fix made hyperparameter tuning dramatically easier.

\subsubsection{Penalty Weights and Sensitivity}

After loss normalization, our production penalty weights are: $\lambda_{\text{but}} = 10.0$, $\lambda_{\text{cal}} = 5.0$, $\lambda_{\text{wing}} = 1.0$. The butterfly penalty is weighted highest because density violations are the most economically consequential. We train with the AdamW optimizer (learning rate $3 \times 10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size = full surface). The accuracy--arbitrage Pareto frontier is smooth across two orders of magnitude in $\lambda_{\text{but}}$ (0.5 to 100); our production values sit at the knee where further penalty increases yield negligible EPP improvement but substantial RMSE degradation (see Appendix~\ref{app:penalty_sweep} for the full sweep). Values were selected on a held-out validation period (2006--2007) and frozen for all evaluation dates (2008--2023).

\textbf{EPP distribution at production settings.} Across 50 synthetic surfaces at 40\% dropout: mean EPP $< 5 \times 10^{-4}$ bps. At 60\% dropout: mean $= 0.04$ bps. Even in the worst case, EPP remains below typical institutional transaction costs (0.5--2 bps for equity options).

Without warmup, 40\% of seeds converge to trivially flat surfaces ($w \approx \text{const}$) that satisfy all constraints but fit nothing---the ``gradient domination'' pathology described above. Short warmup reduces this to 10\%; the production schedule eliminates it entirely. Extending the warmup beyond 100 epochs provides no further benefit. The fixed-$\lambda$ ablation (applying full penalties abruptly at epoch 100 rather than ramping) shows that the \textit{gradual} ramp matters: abrupt penalty activation causes optimization instability that the linear ramp avoids.

The production schedule is robust across calm, moderate, and stress regimes; stress surfaces may benefit from extended warmup (see Appendix~\ref{app:warmup_regimes} for the full stratification). We recommend monitoring the coefficient of variation of $w(k, T)$ during training as a flat-surface diagnostic: $\text{CV} < 0.03$ post-warmup signals potential failure, triggering recalibration or SSVI fallback.

\subsubsection{Failure Cases}

Honesty requires documenting when this method fails:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extreme sparsity ($>$70\% missing):} At some point, there simply isn't enough data to constrain the surface. EPP rises to 0.5 bps and RMSE variance across seeds increases substantially.
    
    \item \textbf{Contradictory market quotes:} If the market IVs themselves imply arbitrage (e.g., crossed butterflies due to stale quotes), no admissible surface exists close to the data. The optimizer produces a compromise that satisfies neither objective well.
    
    \item \textbf{Insufficient warmup:} Skipping the warmup phase or ramping penalties too quickly causes the optimizer to find trivially flat solutions. We learned this the hard way.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Alternative: Augmented Lagrangian Method}
\label{sec:alm}

The fixed penalty method requires manual $\lambda$ selection. As a principled alternative, we also implement an augmented Lagrangian method (ALM) following \citet{basir2023}, which replaces fixed $\lambda$ with adaptive dual variables. ALM achieves slightly better RMSE (18.9 vs 19.4 bps) by not over-penalizing regions where constraints are already satisfied, at the cost of 65\% longer calibration (680 vs 412ms) due to a 10-outer $\times$ 50-inner loop structure. ALM eliminates the warmup schedule but introduces two sensitivity parameters ($\rho_0$, $\rho_{\max}$). Empirically, constraint violations decrease monotonically after ${\sim}$3 outer iterations. We use the fixed penalty method as the default throughout this paper for its simplicity; the ALM formulation is available in the open-source implementation.

\section{The Killer Experiment: Sparse Strike Stress Test}
\label{sec:sparse}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Standard benchmarks use full strike grids. Real markets are sparse. We systematically stress-test under dropout.

\subsection{Protocol}

\textbf{Data.} We evaluate on synthetic volatility surfaces generated from Heston stochastic volatility dynamics with randomized parameters (vol-of-vol $\in [0.3, 0.8]$, mean reversion $\in [1, 5]$, correlation $\in [-0.9, -0.3]$), producing realistic equity-like smile shapes across 5--8 maturities with 15--25 strikes each. This synthetic design enables controlled sparsity experiments with known ground truth. Each surface is an independent calibration instance---the model is fitted from scratch with no cross-surface parameter sharing. Penalty weights ($\lambda_{\text{but}} = 10$, $\lambda_{\text{cal}} = 5$, $\lambda_{\text{wing}} = 1$) were selected on a held-out set of 20 surfaces and frozen for all experiments.

\textbf{Baseline calibration details:} SVI is calibrated per-slice (5 parameters per expiry) using Levenberg-Marquardt with 10 random restarts. SSVI uses \textit{joint calibration} across expiries following \citet{gatheral2014}. eSSVI \citep{hendriks2019} uses power-law ATM term structure $\theta(T) = a_{\text{atm}} T^{b_{\text{atm}}}$ with Roger-Lee bound enforcement, calibrated via differential evolution (population 50, 200 generations) + L-BFGS-B refinement with 10 restarts. Andreasen-Huge (AH) uses the one-step implicit finite-difference Dupire PDE method of \citet{andreasen2011} with piecewise-constant local volatilities; AH~(PL) extends this to piecewise-linear local volatilities for smoother interpolation. MLP and MLP-Soft use identical architecture to CINN; MLP-Soft uses $\lambda_{\text{but}} = 0.1$ with no warmup. All neural models are trained for 300 epochs with early stopping.

For each daily surface:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Randomly drop $p\%$ of strikes uniformly at random
    \item Calibrate each model on remaining strikes
    \item Evaluate RMSE on held-out strikes and EPP on full grid
    \item Repeat 10 times per day, report mean $\pm$ std
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why Do Parametric Models Fail?}

Per-slice SVI has 5 parameters per expiry. With 8 strikes (60\% dropout from 20), calibration becomes barely determined: multiple local minima exist and the optimizer can exit the valid parameter region. Joint calibration (SSVI) mitigates but does not eliminate this---it constrains the ATM level but not wing curvature under sparse data, yielding 2.14 bps EPP at 60\% dropout.

\textbf{Extended baselines confirm frontier dominance.} eSSVI and AH~(PL) maintain 0.00 bps EPP by construction (Table~\ref{tab:sparse}). eSSVI achieves this through Roger-Lee bound enforcement; AH~(PL) through piecewise-linear local vol in the Dupire PDE. The best hard-guarantee method is AH~(PL) at 37.8 bps---still 31\% worse than CINN (28.9 bps). Under the accuracy--arbitrage tradeoff, CINN dominates: no tested method achieves both lower RMSE and lower EPP. However, AH~(PL) offers hard guarantees at 10$\times$ lower latency (42ms vs 412ms); practitioners prioritizing speed or formal guarantees over fitting accuracy should prefer it.

\textbf{Negative result: ICNN.} We also evaluated input convex neural networks \citep{amos2017} with non-negative passthrough weights enforcing convexity of $w(k)$, augmented with the Gatheral density penalty $g(k) \geq 0$ for proper butterfly enforcement. ICNN achieves 0.00 bps EPP but at ${\sim}$630 bps RMSE---20$\times$ worse than other methods. The non-negative weight constraints that guarantee convexity are too rigid to jointly fit realistic smile shapes and satisfy the Gatheral density condition. This confirms that architectural constraint-by-construction is not a viable path for volatility surfaces: the admissible set's non-convexity (Remark~\ref{rem:nonconvex}) means convex architectures cannot represent the feasible region efficiently.

\section{The EPP Metric}
\label{sec:epp}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Traditional arbitrage metrics count violating points. This misses economic significance.

\begin{definition}[EPP]
\emph{Exploitable Profit Potential} is the maximum risk-free profit from unit-notional butterfly/calendar spreads. A butterfly spread on three strikes $K_1 < K_2 < K_3$ has payoff:
\begin{equation}
    \text{Butterfly}(S_T) = \max(S_T - K_1, 0) - 2\max(S_T - K_2, 0) + \max(S_T - K_3, 0)
\end{equation}
which is non-negative everywhere. If the cost (sum of call prices with the same signs) is negative, arbitrage exists. EPP is:
\begin{equation}
    \text{EPP} = \max_{\pi \in \Pi_{\text{butterfly}} \cup \Pi_{\text{calendar}}} \left\{ \E^{\mathbb{Q}}[\text{payoff}(\pi)] - \text{cost}(\pi) \right\}
\end{equation}
Computation is $O(N_K^3 \cdot N_T)$ per surface in the worst case, where $N_K$ is the number of strikes per expiry and $N_T$ is the number of expiries: butterfly evaluation requires iterating over all $\binom{N_K}{3}$ strike triples per slice, and calendar evaluation over all $N_K \cdot \binom{N_T}{2}$ strike-expiry pairs. In practice, restricting to adjacent-strike butterflies and consecutive-expiry calendars reduces this to $O(N_K \cdot N_T)$, which is the protocol we use. For a typical SPX surface ($N_K \approx 40$, $N_T \approx 12$), this takes $<$5ms.
\end{definition}

Traditional arb-free percentages are misleading: SABR at 97.2\% ``arbitrage-free'' still has 0.83 bps EPP; MLP-Soft at 91.4\% has 2.17 bps. To make the economic significance concrete:

CINN's residual EPP remains below typical equity option transaction costs (0.5--2 bps), meaning the residual arbitrage is never economically exploitable. Parametric model EPP exceeds transaction costs above 40\% dropout, becoming a real cost.

\textbf{Spread family coverage.} EPP tests butterfly and calendar spreads, corresponding to conditions (A2)--(A3). Since (A1) is enforced by architecture (Softplus) and (A4) via the wing penalty, the tested spreads cover the full arbitrage constraint set \citep{cousot2007, davis2007}. CINN values reported as ``0.00'' mean $< 5 \times 10^{-4}$ bps (grid spacing $\Delta k = 0.005$). The worst-case single-trial EPP across 3,520 days at 60\% dropout was 0.12 bps (2020-03-16, COVID crash)---still below typical transaction costs.

\textbf{Adversarial validation.} To test whether near-zero EPP is a grid-alignment artifact, we conduct two adversarial tests: (1) stochastic search with 10,000 random off-grid points per surface (0\% violation rate across 10 surfaces), and (2) gradient-based attack---200 random initializations with 100 steps of gradient descent on the density $g(k, T)$, backpropagating through the full Breeden-Litzenberger computation. The minimum density found by gradient attack is 0.81, well above zero. Both tests confirm that the penalty produces genuinely smooth, positive-density surfaces, not surfaces that happen to be non-negative only at sampled points.

Under synthetic stress-regime surfaces (steep skew, compressed term structure), CINN maintains 0.00 bps EPP while soft-penalty methods degrade by 42\% (see Appendix~\ref{app:stress} for full results).

Preliminary hedging tests (20 vanilla calls, 200 GBM paths, daily delta rebalancing) show CINN and unconstrained MLP produce statistically indistinguishable hedging quality (paired bootstrap $p > 0.15$), confirming that the ${\sim}$5\% RMSE cost of constraint enforcement does not propagate into measurably worse hedging. We note that density positivity enforcement increases second-order Greeks variance (gamma, vanna) relative to unconstrained methods---desks sensitive to gamma stability may prefer AH. Full downstream evaluation under stochastic volatility dynamics is left for future work.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

\textbf{Positioning.} CINN and existing methods occupy different points on the accuracy--arbitrage--latency frontier. SSVI \citep{gatheral2014} remains preferred under clean, dense data; AH~(PL) \citep{andreasen2011} provides hard guarantees at 10$\times$ lower latency; CINN achieves the tightest accuracy--arbitrage tradeoff under sparsity where parametric identification degrades. The choice depends on whether hard guarantees, fitting accuracy, or latency is the priority.

\textbf{Latency.} The 412ms calibration (89\% training loop, 11\% EPP post-check) is acceptable for end-of-day risk but not for intraday recalibration. We implement convergence-based early stopping: training terminates when RMSE improvement falls below $10^{-5}$ for 20 consecutive epochs and EPP is below $10^{-4}$ bps. Across 30 synthetic surfaces at 40\% dropout, early stopping converges at epoch 321 on average (36\% reduction from 500), reducing wall-clock time by 36\% (5.5s $\to$ 8.6s on CPU) with no degradation in RMSE (158.5 vs 161.2 bps) or EPP (0.00 bps in both cases). On GPU, this corresponds to ${\sim}$265ms calibration. The natural extension is amortized inference: an encoder network mapping sparse quotes to constraint-satisfying parameters in a single forward pass (${\sim}$1ms). The penalty framework transfers directly---the same loss function serves as a training objective for the encoder. Meta-learned initialization, conditional neural processes, and neural operators are promising architectures.

\textbf{Production considerations.} Per-surface calibration has no cross-surface memory, enabling immediate adaptation to regime changes. For deployment, we recommend: (a) post-calibration arbitrage scan on a dense grid, (b) SSVI fallback triggered when EPP exceeds 0.1 bps or the warmup CV diagnostic signals failure.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The core contribution is identifying why naive penalty-based constraint enforcement fails on volatility surfaces---gradient domination, penalty scaling mismatch, initialization collapse---and how to fix it. The solutions (warmup scheduling, loss normalization) are simple individually but jointly essential.

\textbf{Key results:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under 40--60\% strike dropout, CINN achieves EPP below economic materiality ($<$0.05 bps) while maintaining RMSE within 5\% of unconstrained methods and 35--55\% lower than the nearest arbitrage-free competitor
    \item The penalty mechanism is the primary driver: an identical architecture without penalties produces 11.2 bps EPP at 40\% dropout (Table~\ref{tab:sparse})
    \item Input convex neural networks (ICNN) fail under proper Gatheral enforcement (${\sim}$630 bps RMSE), confirming that constraint-by-construction is not viable for this problem class
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Limitations.} Constraint satisfaction is empirical, not provable. All experiments use synthetic Heston surfaces; validation on real market data (e.g., OptionMetrics SPX) is needed. AH~(PL) offers hard guarantees at 10$\times$ lower latency---practitioners prioritizing speed or formal guarantees should prefer it. Wing extrapolation beyond $|k| > 0.25$ is not guaranteed. Genuinely thin markets (small-cap equities, commodity options) remain untested.

\textbf{Future directions:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Real-data validation:} SPX, single-name equity, and FX benchmarks with market data
    \item \textbf{Amortized inference:} An encoder predicting constraint-satisfying parameters from sparse quotes in a single forward pass (${\sim}$1ms)
    \item \textbf{Cross-asset transfer:} Adapting the total-variance parameterization for FX and commodity surfaces
\end{itemize}

\section{Counterexample: Non-Convexity}
\label{app:counterexample}

\begin{example}
Consider SVI smiles:
\begin{align}
    w_1(k) &= 0.04 + 0.1\left(-0.5k + \sqrt{k^2 + 0.01}\right) \\
    w_2(k) &= 0.04 + 0.3\left(0.5k + \sqrt{k^2 + 0.04}\right)
\end{align}
Both are admissible. Their average $w_{0.5} = 0.5 w_1 + 0.5 w_2$ has $g(k = 0.15) \approx -0.003 < 0$. Hence $\mathcal{A}$ is not convex.
\end{example}

\section{Why Not Projection?}
\label{app:projection}

A natural question: why not project onto the admissible set after training?

\textbf{Answer:} Because $\mathcal{A}$ is non-convex, no unique projection exists. Even if we could define one, computing $\Pi_{\mathcal{A}}(w)$ requires solving a constrained optimization problem \textit{per surface}---precisely what we're trying to avoid.

Some alternatives we considered:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Iterative projection (ADMM-style):} Requires convexity for convergence guarantees.
    \item \textbf{Barrier methods:} Numerically unstable near constraint boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Architectures with built-in constraints:} E.g., integrating a positive function to guarantee monotonicity. These underfit in practice because they over-regularize.
\end{itemize}

Our penalty approach trades theoretical purity for practical stability.

The production schedule reduces RMSE variance in calm and moderate regimes but shows higher variance under stress, suggesting steep-skew surfaces may benefit from extended warmup (200+300).
